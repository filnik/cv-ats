# Action Verbs and Banned Phrases

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global** | **ATS: vendor-neutral**

**Read when:** which verb opens the bullet · is "spearheaded" overclaiming · my resume reads like a model wrote it · which words get me excluded from searches · my real job title is unsearchable

**Not this file:** [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](../guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md) — how to construct the bullet the verb opens, with the metric and the outcome. This document is the word list and nothing else.

## TL;DR

- **Pick the verb that is true, not the verb that is impressive.** A verb is a claim about your role and it is the cheapest claim in the document for an interviewer to test.
- **Choose the category first, the word inside it second.** The expensive error is claiming leadership for delivery work; picking `Built` over `Engineered` costs nothing.
- **Present tense for the current role, past for everything before it. Every bullet opens with the verb** — never a noun phrase, never "Responsible for".
- **An unfalsifiable adjective about yourself is discounted to zero** by a human skimming and by a ranking model reading. The replacement is never a better adjective; it is the instance that demonstrates the quality.
- **The AI tell is the absence of specifics, not the prose style.** Clean formal English is not what gets flagged. A paragraph that would fit any candidate is.
- **Never write "Seeking", "Aspiring", "Looking for" or "Unemployed" on a resume or a profile headline.** Recruiters exclude those strings with Boolean NOT, and exclusion removes you from the result set rather than costing you a rank.

## 1. The verb is a claim, not decoration

Every bullet opens with an assertion about what you personally did. The verb carries that assertion, and it is the one word in the line that a reviewer can challenge without knowing anything about your industry. "Led the migration" invites *who reported to you, and what did you decide?* If the honest answer is "nobody, and I implemented someone else's design", the bullet has cost you more than a vague one would have.

This is why the list below is organised by function rather than alphabetically. Alphabetical lists — which is how most published verb lists are arranged — encourage browsing for the most impressive-sounding word, and browsing for impressiveness is exactly the behaviour that produces the overclaim. **The choice that matters is which of the six categories your work belongs to. Within a category the words are near-interchangeable and the choice is stylistic.**

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** the popular verb lists are presented as if the verb itself moved the outcome — "power verbs increase interview rates", in various phrasings. No field experiment isolates the verb from the bullet it opens, and none of the circulating lists cites one. Choose carefully anyway, for two reasons that need no statistic: the wrong verb makes a claim you cannot defend in an interview, and a vague one wastes the most-read position in the line.

## 2. The list, by what the verb claims

Four separate published lists — two by resume writers, one by a career centre, one by a recruiting firm — were merged to build this, and their overlap was roughly four fifths. That is the useful finding. The consensus verbs are consensus because they are precise, ordinary and unambiguous about role; the words unique to a single list are almost all thesaurus reaches (`Utilised`, `Effectuated`, `Pioneered`) that add register and subtract clarity. What follows is the intersection, deduplicated, with the claim each verb makes stated explicitly.

### 2.1 Leadership and ownership

These claim authority. They are the group where overclaiming is most common and most expensive.

| Verb | The claim it makes | Do not use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Led** | You had authority over the work and the outcome was yours | You were one contributor among several, or the decisions were someone else's |
| **Directed** | Formal authority over people or a function | You had influence but no reporting line |
| **Spearheaded** | You initiated it and drove it forward, with or without formal authority | You joined something already running |
| **Founded** | It did not exist before you | A team that predated you under another name |
| **Established** | You created something durable — a practice, a process, a function that outlived the project | A one-off initiative that ended with you |
| **Orchestrated** | Coordination across groups you did not control | Two people inside your own team |
| **Chaired** | You ran a standing forum and set its agenda | You attended it, even reliably |
| **Owned** | Named accountability, including for the failures | Accountability was genuinely shared |

### 2.2 Building and delivery

These claim that a thing exists which did not exist before. They are the safest group: the artefact is the evidence, and it is checkable.

| Verb | The claim it makes | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Built** | You made the thing with your hands | The plainest and the most defensible. When in doubt, this one |
| **Delivered** | It reached the people it was for, on a commitment | Implies a deadline or a scope you were held to |
| **Shipped** | It went to production or to customers | Stronger than `Built` because it claims the last mile |
| **Implemented** | You realised a specification that existed | Correctly modest when the design was not yours |
| **Deployed** | You put it into a running environment | Operational, not design work |
| **Launched** | First release to a real audience, with the go-live attached | Reserve for genuine launches; overused for internal rollouts |
| **Engineered** | Deliberate technical design under constraints | Only when there were real constraints to name |
| **Developed** | Broad, safe, slightly weak | Fine as a second or third verb, weak as your best one |
| **Architected** | You set the structure others built inside | A senior claim. Expect to defend the trade-offs you rejected |

### 2.3 Improvement and optimisation

These claim a delta. Every one of them raises the question *from what to what* — so each of these verbs obliges you to supply a number, or an explicit substitute for one. See [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](../patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md) when the number does not exist.

| Verb | The claim it makes | Do not use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduced** | A quantity went down and you caused it | You cannot name the quantity |
| **Improved** | Something got better on a stated dimension | The dimension is unstated — this is the vaguest verb in the group |
| **Optimised** | You tuned an existing system against a target | Nothing was measured before or after |
| **Increased** | A quantity went up and you caused it | The rise was market-driven or someone else's release |
| **Accelerated** | Something got faster — a process, a build, a cycle | Only speed changed in your description of it but not in fact |
| **Streamlined** | Steps were removed | No step was actually removed |
| **Consolidated** | Many became fewer — systems, vendors, repositories, reports | Nothing was retired |
| **Automated** | Human work stopped being done by humans | A human still does it, faster |
| **Migrated** | Content or workload moved from one system to another, intact | The old system is still the system of record |
| **Refactored** | Internal structure changed, behaviour did not | Behaviour changed too — then it is `Rebuilt` |
| **Rebuilt** | You replaced it, deliberately, and it works | The replacement never shipped |

### 2.4 Analysis and research

These claim you produced knowledge. They are weak on their own and strong when the bullet ends in a decision that followed.

| Verb | The claim it makes |
|---|---|
| **Analysed** | You worked over data and drew a conclusion from it |
| **Evaluated** | You compared options against criteria you can state |
| **Assessed** | You judged a state of affairs — risk, readiness, capability |
| **Diagnosed** | You found the cause of a specific failure |
| **Investigated** | You pursued an open question with no guaranteed answer |
| **Modelled** | You built a representation that predicted something |
| **Benchmarked** | You measured against an external or prior reference point |

`Diagnosed` and `Benchmarked` are the two worth reaching for: both imply a method, and neither is claimable without one.

### 2.5 Enablement and influence

These are the register for work done through other people without authority over them — the hardest work to evidence and the most common thing senior candidates under-describe.

| Verb | The claim it makes |
|---|---|
| **Unblocked** | Something was stuck, you moved it, and it was not your work that was stuck |
| **Facilitated** | You ran a process that produced a decision you did not make |
| **Coordinated** | You kept parallel work in step across groups |
| **Negotiated** | Two parties wanted different things and you closed the gap |
| **Advised** | Your recommendation was taken by someone senior to you |
| **Mentored** | Sustained one-to-one development of named people |
| **Trained** | You taught a defined skill to a defined group |
| **Championed** | You pushed an unpopular position until it was adopted |
| **Aligned** | Parties who disagreed ended up committed to one plan |

`Championed` and `Aligned` are soft enough to read as filler unless the bullet says who disagreed and what changed. Use them with the resistance named.

### 2.6 Recovery and rescue

The underused group, and the right register for a role that was mostly firefighting. If your last two years were incidents, inherited systems and cleaning up other people's decisions, this is your vocabulary — not the delivery vocabulary, which will make a chaotic job sound like a tidy one and lose you the credit for the hard part.

| Verb | The claim it makes |
|---|---|
| **Rescued** | It was failing, and it is not failing now |
| **Stabilised** | The bleeding stopped — incidents, churn, cost, attrition |
| **Recovered** | Something lost came back: data, a customer, a deadline, a quarter |
| **Turned around** | A trend reversed direction under you |
| **Remediated** | You closed findings someone else raised — audit, security, compliance |

These verbs carry a premise: something was broken. State what it was, briefly and without blaming a named person. "Inherited a service with weekly production incidents; stabilised it to none in six weeks" is a stronger opening than any leadership verb, because the before-state does the work that an adjective cannot.

## 3. Choosing between two verbs that both fit

**Prefer the accurate verb over the impressive one.** This is not modesty advice; it is risk management. An interviewer probes the strongest claim on the page first, because that is where the information is. A `Led` you cannot substantiate is the single easiest thing in a resume to puncture, and the puncture costs you more than the word ever bought — you have now demonstrated that your document inflates, and everything else on it is re-read in that light.

The compensation is that **the accurate verb is usually the more impressive one anyway, because it is specific**. Compare:

```text
Led a project to improve the checkout system
Rebuilt checkout on a new payments provider, cutting failed transactions by half
```

`Led` claims more and says less. `Rebuilt` is a smaller claim about authority and a much larger claim about capability, and it is the one that survives a follow-up question. The same holds for `Migrated`, `Diagnosed`, `Stabilised` and `Negotiated`: they name what happened, so the reader does not have to take an adjective on trust.

Two working rules:

1. **If you would hesitate to say the sentence out loud to the colleague who actually led it, change the verb.** That test is nearly perfect and takes a second.
2. **When two verbs are both true, take the one that constrains the bullet more.** `Reduced` forces a number out of you. `Improved` lets you get away without one, which is why it is the most common weak verb on any resume and why it should be your last choice, not your first.

## 4. The three mechanical rules

These are not stylistic preferences. Each one fixes a specific failure.

**Tense.** Present tense for the role you currently hold, past tense for every prior role. Mixed tense inside a single role reads as carelessness; past tense in a current role reads as though you have already left, and reviewers notice. If a project inside your current role is finished, past tense for that bullet alone is correct and normal.

**Position.** The verb is the first word of the bullet. Never a noun phrase (`Responsibility for the deployment pipeline…`), never a participle (`Working with stakeholders to…`), never a scene-setter (`As part of a cross-functional team, I…`). The reader is skimming the left edge of the page; whatever sits in the first two words is doing all of the work.

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, covertly timed via session recording, 2023):** recruiters spent 17 seconds on a short one-page resume, 27 on a mid-length one and 46 on a long one, with the fastest observed pass at 12 seconds. That is a skim, and a skim reads openings.

**Repetition.** Do not use the same verb more than twice in one document. Three `Led`s in a column do not read as consistency; they read as a small vocabulary, and worse, they flatten three different kinds of contribution into one word. This is the single most common reason to reach into a neighbouring category — and reaching for a synonym you are not entitled to is how the overclaim in section 3 happens. If you have run out of accurate leadership verbs, that is information: most of the work was probably delivery or enablement, and it should be described as such.

## 5. Banned group one: empty self-assessment

| Phrase | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Responsible for / Duties included | Describes the job, not you. Two candidates with identical duties are indistinguishable |
| Team player | Unfalsifiable. Nobody has ever written "difficult to work with" |
| Detail-oriented | Same, and it is routinely written by people whose resume contains a typo |
| Results-oriented / Results-driven | Orientation towards results is not a result |
| Self-starter / Motivated / Hard-working / Go-getter | Assertions about your interior life, unverifiable from outside it |
| Passionate about | The most-mocked phrase in the field and still the most-used |
| Think outside the box / Synergy / Dynamic professional | Dead metaphor, corporate noise, and a self-award respectively |
| Proven track record | Announces evidence instead of providing it. The bullets underneath are the track record, or nothing is |

**Why this group fails, precisely.** Each one asserts a quality without an instance. A human reviewer discounts an unfalsifiable claim to zero because it carries no information — every applicant writes it, so it cannot separate anyone. A ranking model discounts it for the same reason and one more: these phrases occur with near-uniform frequency across the whole applicant pool, so they contribute nothing that distinguishes your document from the others in the queue.

**The replacement is never a better adjective.** There is no strong way to say "detail-oriented". The replacement is the instance:

```text
Detail-oriented professional with a proven track record of quality
Cut post-release defects from 14 per release to 2 by adding a pre-merge check
```

The second sentence never claims to be detail-oriented, and it is the only one of the two that proves it. Full treatment of how to turn a quality into an instance is in [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](../guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md).

## 6. Banned group two: the AI-generated tells

| Phrase | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| "I am writing to express my strong interest in…" | Cover letter opening |
| "I am excited about the opportunity to contribute to…" | Cover letter, summary |
| "leverage my skills" / "leverage my expertise" | Summary, cover letter |
| "thrive in a fast-paced environment" | Summary |
| "align with your mission and values" | Cover letter |
| "I believe I would be a great fit" | Cover letter close |

These now carry a cost beyond blandness, because reviewers in 2026 read them as evidence that a model wrote the document and the candidate did not read it.

> **Study (Greenhouse 2025 workforce research, sample not disclosed, citing a Gartner projection):** 22% of active job seekers admit using bots to apply automatically, rising to 31% among Gen Z, and Gartner projects that by 2028, 25% of job applicants will be fake. Vendor research with a commercial interest in detection tooling — treat the direction as real and the magnitude as soft.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** the widely quoted figures for what share of hiring managers reject applications they believe were AI-written vary between surveys by a wide margin, and no primary source with a disclosed sample and instrument has been located for this knowledge base. The behaviour is real and reported by practitioners; the number is not established. Do not repeat one.

### 6.1 What the tell actually is

This matters more than the phrase list, because the phrase list is not the mechanism.

**The tell is not prose style.** A non-native English writer producing clean, formal, slightly stiff English is not what gets flagged — that register has existed in applications for decades and reviewers read it as care, not as automation. Advice to "write worse English so it looks human" is wrong and actively harmful to anyone applying across a language border.

**The tell is the absence of specifics.** What marks a document as machine-written is that nothing in it could only have been written by this candidate: no numbers, no named systems, no constraint that was actually binding, no reference to this company that could not be pasted into an application to a different one. A model asked to write a cover letter with no facts produces fluent, correct, interchangeable text — and interchangeability is the signal.

**The countermeasure follows directly.** Write content no model would generate without you:

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "leverage my skills in modern web technologies" | the actual stack, with versions and the part you owned |
| "thrive in a fast-paced environment" | the constraint that made it fast — a launch date, a regulator, a headcount cap |
| "align with your mission" | one specific thing the company shipped or published, and why it bears on your work |
| "delivered significant improvements" | the before number and the after number |
| "collaborated with cross-functional stakeholders" | which functions, on what decision, and what they wanted that you did not |

A trade-off you actually made is the strongest anti-tell available, because it requires you to say what you gave up — and a model with no access to your history cannot invent one that survives a follow-up question. Cover-letter-specific handling of the same problem is in [guides/06-cover-letters.md](../guides/06-cover-letters.md).

## 7. Banned group three: the words in recruiters' NOT clauses

This is the most concretely costly group and the least known, because the cost is invisible to you.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** LinkedIn's search documentation describes the Boolean operators available in the search box — quoted phrases, parentheses, AND, OR and NOT. `NOT` excludes every profile containing the term. The same operator set is standard in the candidate-search box of every major ATS.

| Word | Why a recruiter excludes it | What to write instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Seeking** | Filters out headlines announcing availability rather than capability | Your role and specialism |
| **Aspiring** | The single clearest junior marker in the vocabulary | The thing you actually do now |
| **Looking for** | Same as `Seeking`, in longer form | Nothing — delete the sentence |
| **Trainee** | Excluded to remove entry-level profiles from senior searches | The accurate title, if it is accurate |
| **Intern** | Same | Keep it only where it is factually your title |
| **Student** | Excluded from experienced-hire searches wholesale | Your degree in the education section, where it belongs |
| **Unemployed** | Nobody searches for it; several recruiters exclude it | Nothing. A gap is handled with dates, not with a label |

**How the damage works.** These are not ranking penalties. A `NOT` clause removes your record from the result set entirely: you are not ranked low, you are absent, and absence is indistinguishable from never having existed. Nothing tells you it happened. A recruiter searching `"backend engineer" AND Kotlin NOT (student OR intern OR aspiring)` — a routine query when a senior requisition is drowning in junior applications — will never see you, no matter how well the rest of the profile matches.

**The headline case is the worst one.** A profile headline reading "Seeking new opportunities in software development" occupies the highest-visibility text you own, on the surface recruiters search most, and it spends that space on a word that disqualifies you from result sets while telling a reader nothing about what you can do. Replace it with the role and the specialism: "Backend Engineer — Kotlin, distributed systems, payments". The dedicated availability signals exist as *settings*, not as text you type into your name or headline — see [guides/07-linkedin.md](../guides/07-linkedin.md) for which of the two settings to use and what each one exposes.

Two honest qualifications. First, `Intern`, `Trainee` and `Student` are only banned when they are not accurate; where the title is real, write it, because misrepresenting a title is a worse problem than being filtered. Second, this rule is derived from how recruiters describe their own queries rather than from a published dataset of Boolean strings — no vendor publishes what its customers type. The mechanism is documented; the prevalence is practitioner report.

## 8. Title vocabulary

Titles are searched more than almost anything else on your document, which makes the title string a retrieval decision rather than a matter of accuracy alone.

> **Study (n=384 HR professionals directly involved in hiring, random sampling, 2025):** job titles are the third most-used filter inside the ATS at 55.3%, behind skills at 76.4% and educational background at 59.7%.

Three rules, in priority order:

1. **Use the market-standard title, because retrieval runs on the string people search for.** `Software Engineer` is searched; `Code Wrangler`, `Digital Ninja` and `Growth Hacker` are not. An internally invented title is a title that exists in exactly one company's vocabulary, and a search will not find it.
2. **Where the official title is genuinely unconventional, write the standard one and put the internal one in parentheses** — `Senior Backend Engineer (internally: Staff Technologist II)`. This keeps you findable and keeps the record honest, which matters when a background check compares your document against an employment reference. The parsing-level version of this rule, including why the title and the company must sit on separate lines, is in [references/parsing-rules.md](parsing-rules.md).
3. **Never invent a past title.** This is a different act from the first two and it is not a vocabulary choice — translating `Staff Technologist II` into `Senior Backend Engineer` describes the same job in the language of the market, whereas promoting yourself from Engineer to Engineering Manager describes a job you did not hold. The first is translation. The second is misrepresentation, it is checkable by a phone call, and it ends candidacies at the reference stage rather than the screening one.

Where the market title is genuinely ambiguous across companies — `Product Manager` at a five-person startup and at a bank are different jobs — the fix is the bullets underneath, not a modified title.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I used 'Led' because I was the most senior person nearby" | The first interview question is who reported to you and what you decided. The word costs more than it bought |
| "I varied my verbs so the resume wouldn't be repetitive" | Varying inside a category is fine; varying across categories silently upgraded delivery work into leadership claims |
| "Every bullet starts with 'Responsible for' — it's accurate" | It describes the vacancy, not you. The next candidate's identical duties read identically |
| "I opened with 'Detail-oriented professional with a proven track record'" | Two unfalsifiable claims in seven words. A reviewer discounts both to nothing and a ranking model finds nothing distinguishing in either |
| "My headline says 'Seeking new opportunities'" | Recruiters exclude `seeking` with Boolean NOT. You are removed from the result set, not ranked lower, and nothing tells you |
| "I'm a student, so my headline says Aspiring Developer" | `Aspiring` is excluded from experienced-hire searches, and the word claims nothing. Write what you build |
| "I rewrote my letter in simpler English so it wouldn't look AI-written" | The tell was never the prose. It was that no sentence named a system, a number or a constraint. Simpler English with the same emptiness reads exactly the same |
| "I kept my real title, Growth Ninja, because that's what it said on my contract" | Nobody searches that string. Write the market title and put the internal one in parentheses |
| "I promoted my last title slightly — everyone does" | It is checked at the reference stage, where a discrepancy ends the process regardless of how well the interviews went |
| "I used 'Improved' throughout because I don't have numbers" | `Improved` is the one verb in its category that lets you avoid the delta. That is why it is weak — pick the verb that forces the number, then go and find it |
