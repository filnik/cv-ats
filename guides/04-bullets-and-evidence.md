# Bullets: Writing Evidence a Machine and a Human Both Accept

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global** | **ATS: vendor-neutral, with one documented AI layer**

**Read when:** your bullets read like duties · X-Y-Z versus CAR versus CART · which numbers to use · everything starts with "responsible for" · past or present tense · can I estimate a figure I never measured

**Not this file:** [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](../patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md) — for the case where you have looked and the numbers do not exist. This document assumes a number is recoverable.

## TL;DR

- **The five named formulas are one formula.** Outcome, magnitude, method. Learn it once and stop shopping for acronyms.
- **Lead with the outcome.** The reader is skimming the left edge of the page and decides in four or five words whether to read the rest of the line. Spend those words on what changed, not on what you touched.
- **Every bullet opens with a verb. Present tense for the current role, past for everything before it.** No pronouns, no participles, no "Responsible for".
- **Choose the metric by what it measures — scale, money, time, quality, people, adoption — not by what sounds impressive.** Everyone's work touches at least three of those six.
- **No field experiment isolates quantification.** Every number circulating about its effect is untraceable. Quantify anyway, because an evaluator that cites its evidence has nothing to cite in an adjective.
- **A number you cannot defend is worse than no number.** False precision is the same fabrication signature on a resume as it is in an article.

## 1. Five formulas, one formula

The literature on resume bullets is a naming problem wearing the costume of a technique problem. Five formulas circulate. Each has an acronym, a set of advocates, and an article explaining why it beats the others. A reader who meets them in sequence concludes there is a choice to make.

There is not. Here they are as published.

| Formula | Where it comes from | Its literal shape | What it claims to add |
|---|---|---|---|
| **X-Y-Z** | Hiring guidance from a large tech employer, promoted ever since as the FAANG standard | "Accomplished **[X]** as measured by **[Y]** by doing **[Z]**" | Makes the measure a required slot rather than a nice-to-have |
| **CAR** | The resume-writing corpus, taught as challenge, action, result | Challenge → action → result, then **inverted so the result is written first** | The before-state, and the instruction to lead with the result |
| **CART** | An extension of CAR | Challenge → action → result → **transformation or takeaway** | Explicitly reusable in a cover letter, an interview answer and a performance review |
| **Four-step (sometimes LEG)** | Career-services teaching | Action verb + contribution and skills used + result + metrics | Names the skills used as a separate slot |
| **Action + Tool + Metric** | Engineering-resume guidance | "Built **X** using **Y**, achieving **Z**" | Forces the named technology into the line |

Now decompose them. Every one resolves into the same three elements, and there are only three.

| Element | The question it answers | X-Y-Z | CAR / CART | Four-step | Action + Tool + Metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome** | What changed? | X | R | result | the impact clause |
| **Magnitude** | By how much? | Y | folded inside R | metrics | the quantifier |
| **Method** | By what means? | Z | A | contribution and skills used | action + tool |

The differences that survive that table are two, and both are small:

1. **Ordering.** X-Y-Z and CAR-taught-properly put the outcome first. The four-step formula and Action-plus-Tool-plus-Metric put the method first. That is the whole disagreement.
2. **Whether the method is compulsory.** X-Y-Z makes it a slot you must fill. CAR lets a result stand alone if it is strong enough.

Two residues do not fit the three elements, and both are worth keeping even though neither is a fourth element. CAR's **C** is the before-state — the thing that was broken, slow or missing — which is context rather than a claim, and which frequently does more work than any adjective could. CART's **T** is not a part of the sentence at all; it is a *practice*: write the achievement once, in this shape, and reuse the same material in the cover letter, the interview answer and the next performance review. That practice is the subject of [patterns/brag-document.md](../patterns/brag-document.md).

**STAR is the interview relative of the same skeleton.** Situation, task, action, result — outcome last, context first. That ordering is correct when you are speaking and the listener is obliged to hear you to the end. It is wrong in a document nobody is obliged to finish. Do not write STAR bullets on a resume.

**The recommendation: use the three elements, and lead with the outcome.** The rest of this document is that formula taught once, the catalogue of things the magnitude can be, and the two ways it goes wrong.

## 2. The formula, and why the outcome goes first

### 2.1 The shape

```text
<Verb> <what changed> <by how much>, by <means> [over <period> | despite <constraint>]
```

Three slots are mandatory: the verb-plus-outcome, the magnitude, and the means. The bracketed tail is optional and earns its place only when the period or the constraint is itself impressive — six weeks, or with no additional headcount, or during a platform migration.

The verb is not a fourth element; it is how the outcome is stated. Which verb, and the claim each one makes about your authority, is [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md). Choose the accurate verb rather than the impressive one, and note that the improvement verbs — `Reduced`, `Increased`, `Accelerated` — oblige you to supply a magnitude by their grammar alone.

### 2.2 Why the outcome and not the method

Because the reader is skimming, and the outcome is the part that earns the next second of attention.

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, covertly timed via session recording, 2023):** recruiters spent 17 seconds on a short one-page resume, 27 on a mid-length one and 46 on a long one; the fastest observed pass was 12 seconds. The timing was covert, which removes the demand characteristic that inflated earlier eye-tracking exercises.

Whatever number you prefer inside that range, it is a skim, and a skim reads the beginning of lines. A method-first bullet spends its most-read position on the input — the framework, the tool, the team you sat in — and the input is the least differentiating thing on the page, because every other applicant for the same job used something similar.

The common objection is that the method is the keyword, and the keyword must be found. It must, and it will be: retrieval is string matching and semantic matching over the whole document, not over the first four words of each line. Position matters to the human; presence matters to the search. Put the term in the same sentence and you have satisfied both. The mechanics of that are [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](05-keywords-and-tailoring.md).

**The test.** Read the bullet and ask *so what?* If the bullet answers its own question, the outcome is present. If answering requires you to add a sentence out loud, that sentence is the bullet and what you wrote is the preamble.

### 2.3 One bullet, five drafts

```text
1. Responsible for the CI pipeline and build tooling
2. Improved build performance across the mobile team
3. Optimised the Gradle build using build caching and module splitting
4. Cut mobile CI build time from 22 minutes to 4 by introducing
   remote build caching and splitting the monolith into 9 modules
5. Cut mobile CI build time from 22 minutes to 4, recovering roughly
   3 engineer-days a week across a team of 14, by introducing remote
   build caching and splitting the monolith into 9 modules
```

Draft 1 is a job description. Draft 2 has a verb and no magnitude, so it asserts rather than claims. Draft 3 is method-first: everything after the verb is input, and the line never says what happened. Draft 4 is a correct bullet. Draft 5 is the same bullet with the *second-order* outcome attached — the minutes are the measurement, the recovered engineer-days are the thing the employer buys — and it is the version worth the extra line.

Do not go to a sixth draft. Adding a third metric to a line that already carries two is where bullets start reading as inventory.

## 3. Bullet grammar

These are mechanical rules, not stylistic preferences. Each fixes a specific failure.

**Voice: every bullet opens with a verb.** Never a noun phrase (`Responsibility for the deployment pipeline…`), never a participle (`Working with stakeholders to…`), never a scene-setter (`As part of a cross-functional team, I…`). No first-person pronouns anywhere in the section — the document is understood to be about you, and `I` spends a word saying so.

**Tense: present for the role you currently hold, past for every prior role.** Past tense in a current role reads as though you have already left. Mixed tense inside one role reads as carelessness. The one legitimate exception is a finished project inside your current role: past tense for that bullet alone is correct and nobody blinks.

**Length: one line, two at the absolute maximum.** A three-line bullet is a paragraph wearing a bullet's clothes and it is skipped, which means the achievement inside it was never read. If a bullet will not compress, it is usually two achievements pretending to be one.

**Count: weight the recent role.** Four to six bullets for the current or most recent position, three or four for the one before, one or two for anything more than roughly ten years back. A uniform block of five bullets per role tells the reader you have not decided what matters.

**Digits, not words.** Write `9 modules`, not `nine modules`. Digits interrupt a skim; spelled numbers dissolve into the prose around them. This is the one place where a resume should ignore ordinary prose style.

**Consistency of punctuation.** Full stops on all bullets or on none. Mixed terminal punctuation is the single most common proofreading tell, and a reviewer who notices it starts reading for errors instead of for content.

Verb repetition, the six verb families, and the phrases that get discounted by human and model alike are [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md).

## 4. The metric catalogue

Most published metric lists are organised by domain: metrics for marketers, metrics for engineers, metrics for project managers. Those lists are unusable the moment your work crosses a boundary, which it always does. Organise instead by **what the number measures**. There are six families, and almost every job touches at least three of them.

The third column is the one people skip and the one that actually unblocks the writing. If you cannot name the system where a number lives, you are about to invent it — go and look instead. Making that lookup a habit rather than an annual panic is [patterns/brag-document.md](../patterns/brag-document.md).

### 4.1 Scale — how much of it there was

Scale evidences the blast radius of your decisions. It is the family to reach for when the outcome was correctness or survival rather than improvement: nothing got better, but a great deal did not break.

| Metric | What it evidences | Where the number lives |
|---|---|---|
| Users, accounts, seats, subscribers | The size of the population your work touched | Product analytics, billing system |
| Requests, events, messages per second or per day | The load your design survived | Monitoring dashboard, load balancer, gateway logs |
| Records, rows, documents, files processed | Data volume handled | Warehouse, batch job logs |
| Transactions, orders, claims, tickets | Commercial or operational throughput | Payments console, service desk |
| Countries, markets, locales, sites, brands | Breadth of rollout | Release notes, configuration repository |
| Peak or concurrent load | Headroom under stress | Incident review, capacity plan |

```text
Rebuilt the ingestion path for a service handling 40M events a day
across 11 markets, holding p95 latency under 200ms through a 3x
seasonal peak
Migrated 2.4M customer records to the new billing platform with zero
reconciliation discrepancies over the first two closes
```

### 4.2 Money — what it was worth

The family every hiring manager translates into first, and the one most candidates avoid because they believe it belongs to sales. It does not. Cost saved is money, and so is engineering time recovered once you price it.

| Metric | What it evidences | Where the number lives |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue generated or influenced | Direct commercial contribution | CRM, finance reporting |
| Cost reduced or avoided | Efficiency with a currency attached | Cloud billing console, vendor invoices |
| Budget or spend owned | Financial scope and trust | Procurement records, your own approvals |
| Contract value won, renewed or retained | Commercial durability | CRM, renewals report |
| Margin, unit cost, cost per transaction | Economic understanding of the product | Finance model |
| Recovered write-offs, fraud or leakage prevented | Loss you stopped | Risk or finance reporting |

Two rules keep this family honest. **Say "influenced" when you influenced it** — attaching a whole deal to yourself when you built one component of it is the fastest way to lose an interview. And **when the absolute figure is confidential, use a ratio or an order of magnitude**: "cut annual cloud spend by a third on a seven-figure bill" discloses nothing and evidences everything.

```text
Cut annual AWS spend by €310k (31%) by rightsizing 40 over-provisioned
instances and moving cold storage to lifecycle policies
Owned a €1.2M tooling budget across 4 vendors; consolidated to 2 at a
€180k annual saving with no loss of coverage
```

### 4.3 Time — how much faster

The family with the best evidence behind it inside a company, because the before-state is nearly always recorded somewhere. It is also the family where the second-order outcome is most available: minutes saved multiply by the number of people who used to wait.

| Metric | What it evidences | Where the number lives |
|---|---|---|
| Latency, response time, p95 or p99 | Engineering quality under load | Monitoring, performance dashboard |
| Cycle time, lead time, time to first review | Flow through a process you changed | Issue tracker, delivery metrics |
| Deployment frequency, release cadence | Confidence and automation | CI history, release log |
| Time to market, time to launch | Delivery against a commercial window | Roadmap, launch records |
| Onboarding time, ramp time, time to first value | How much friction you removed | HR system, product analytics |
| Hours recovered per week or per cycle | Human time released | Timesheets, or an honest count times a rate |

```text
Reduced release cycle from fortnightly to daily by automating the
regression suite, taking mean lead time from 9 days to 14 hours
Halved new-engineer ramp time (6 weeks to 3) by rewriting the local
environment setup as a single scripted bootstrap
```

### 4.4 Quality — how much less went wrong

The natural family for maintenance, platform, operations and compliance work — roles that are chronically under-described because the outcome is an absence. An absence is measurable. Measure it.

| Metric | What it evidences | Where the number lives |
|---|---|---|
| Defect rate, escaped bugs, change failure rate | Whether your delivery holds up | Issue tracker, CI history |
| Incident count, severity mix, mean time to recovery | Operational reliability | Incident tool, on-call records |
| Uptime or availability against a stated target | Whether the commitment was met | Status page, service level report |
| Test coverage, static analysis findings closed | Investment in durability — weak alone, good with a defect delta beside it | CI reports |
| Customer satisfaction, net promoter, support contact rate | Quality as the customer experienced it | Support platform, survey tool |
| Audit or security findings remediated | Closure of an externally raised risk | Audit report, vulnerability tracker |

Coverage on its own is the weakest metric in this document — it measures effort, not outcome. Never write it without the consequence beside it.

```text
Took the payments service from 6 Sev-1 incidents a quarter to zero
across two consecutive quarters by adding idempotency keys and a
replay-safe retry path
Raised critical-path test coverage from 34% to 81%, cutting escaped
defects per release from 12 to 3
```

### 4.5 People — who grew, and who stayed

The family that distinguishes a senior individual contributor from a lead, and the one where inflation is most easily caught, because team sizes are checkable in a single reference call.

| Metric | What it evidences | Where the number lives |
|---|---|---|
| Team size, direct reports, dotted-line reports | Span of responsibility — state which kind | Org chart |
| Hires made, offer acceptance, time to hire | Ability to build the team, not just run it | Recruiting system |
| Promotions of people you managed or mentored | Whether people grew under you | HR records, your own notes |
| Retention or regretted attrition | Whether people stayed — the hardest people metric to fake | HR reporting |
| Mentees, apprentices, interns converted | Sustained development of named individuals | Your own notes, programme records |
| Reviewers, contributors, or teams onboarded to something you built | Influence without authority | Repository history, adoption dashboard |

```text
Grew the mobile team from 4 to 11 over 18 months, hiring 6 engineers
and promoting 2 to senior, with no regretted attrition
Mentored 5 engineers through the mid-to-senior transition; 4 were
promoted within 12 months
```

### 4.6 Adoption — whether anyone actually used it

The family that separates shipped from used, and the one that answers the question a hiring manager silently asks about every project on the page: *did it matter?*

| Metric | What it evidences | Where the number lives |
|---|---|---|
| Activation rate, first-run completion | Whether new users got value | Product analytics |
| Feature uptake, share of eligible users | Whether the thing you built was wanted | Product analytics, feature flags |
| Retention or repeat use — of the product, not of staff | Durable value rather than a launch spike | Cohort analysis |
| Engagement: sessions, depth, frequency | Behaviour change | Product analytics |
| Internal adoption: teams, repositories, services migrated | Whether a platform investment landed | Migration tracker, dependency graph |
| Conversion at a named step | A funnel you moved | Analytics, experimentation platform |

"Retention" means two unrelated things across families 4.5 and 4.6. Say which one you mean in the words of the bullet — *customer retention*, *staff retention* — because a reader who has to guess will guess the less impressive one.

```text
Drove internal adoption of the design system from 3 to 27 services in
9 months, retiring 4 duplicate component libraries
Raised week-4 retention from 19% to 27% by rebuilding onboarding
around a single activation moment, measured on a 40k-user cohort
```

### 4.7 Choosing which metric to use

- **One metric per bullet. Two when the second is the consequence of the first** — the minutes and the engineer-days in draft 5 of section 2.3. Three is inventory.
- **Prefer the metric nearest to what the employer buys.** A latency number is good; a latency number that a business cares about because checkout abandonment fell is better. Move one step toward the money if the step is honest, and stop if it is not.
- **Prefer a delta over an absolute**, unless the absolute is the point. `From 22 minutes to 4` beats `by 82%`, because it shows the baseline and lets the reader do their own arithmetic. A percentage without a baseline is the easiest figure on a resume to inflate, and reviewers know it.
- **Do not repeat a family down the page.** Six consecutive cost-reduction bullets describe a narrow contributor even when each one is impressive.

## 5. What the evidence for quantification actually is

This section exists because the recommendation you have just read is the most confidently repeated advice in the field and the worst-supported. Saying so is not a caveat. It changes what argument you should trust.

**Every source in this field recommends quantification, and no field experiment isolates it.** There is no randomised trial in which the same resume was submitted with and without numbers to real openings, and no observational study that separates quantification from the seniority and the competence that tend to come with it.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** the most-repeated figure in resume advice is that quantified achievements increase callbacks by 40%. It is attributed to a company that is now defunct, whose blog analysed only its own paying customers, and in whose surviving posts the specific claim does not appear. The related figures are worse: "3.2x more likely to get callbacks", "2x fixation time on quantified bullets", "89% of recruiters identify quantified achievements in under 10 seconds" — none has any locatable source at all. The claim-by-claim tracing is in [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md).

The tell in that cluster is the shape rather than the size. A real finding has one value and one method; a fabricated one circulates at several values because nobody is anchored to anything.

**Quantify anyway.** The argument is mechanistic, and a mechanism you can inspect is stronger than a statistic you cannot trace.

1. **A metric is verifiable.** "Reduced build time from 22 minutes to 4" is a claim that can be probed and defended. "Improved build performance" is not a claim; it is a self-assessment with a verb in front of it. Only the first can be confirmed, and only something confirmable can be credited.
2. **A metric is comparable.** A reviewer holding several hundred applications for one opening is not evaluating you; they are ranking. Adjectives do not rank against each other — every candidate's are positive — and quantities do.
3. **A metric gives an evaluator something to cite.** This is the one that has changed since 2024, and it is the strongest of the three.

### 5.1 The citation problem

Modern AI screening layers do not produce a mystical fit score out of nothing. The best-documented of them evaluates named criteria and shows its working.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** in Ashby's screening design, a recruiter defines criteria, each a title plus a prompt written as if instructing a junior recruiter. The model evaluates each criterion **independently** and returns **Meets, Does not meet, or Undecided**, with narrative reasoning **and citations into the source text of the resume**. There is no holistic ranking and no numerical rating of the candidate.

Read that as a specification for the sentence you are writing. A criterion is a question of fact — *has three years of production Kotlin*, *has owned a budget*, *has shipped to a regulated market* — and the model must point at the words that answer it.

- A bullet containing a dated role, a named system and a quantity **contains a citable span**. The verdict can be "meets", and the reasoning quotes you.
- A bullet containing "extensive experience with", "proven track record of" or "passionate about" **contains nothing citable**. The honest verdict is "does not meet" or "undecided", not because the model is hostile but because there is no evidence in the sentence.
- Because criteria are evaluated **independently**, a brilliant answer to one does not compensate for silence on another. Coverage beats brilliance. Address every stated requirement somewhere concrete rather than perfecting three bullets and leaving a fourth requirement unmentioned.

That design makes the point better than any statistic could: **the ranking layer is a citation engine, and an adjective is not quotable.** What each vendor's layer does, and which of them gate rather than sort, is [references/ats-catalog.md](../references/ats-catalog.md).

The same property serves the human reader, who is running the identical procedure at speed and with less patience. This is the rare case where writing for the machine and writing for the person are not a trade-off at all.

## 6. Four ways a bullet fails

Each of these survives on a resume because it feels like it is describing work. None of them is.

### 6.1 Responsibility instead of outcome

The most common failure and the easiest to spot: the bullet describes the job you were given rather than what happened because you held it. Any successor in the same seat could write the identical line.

```text
Before: Responsible for maintaining backend systems and ensuring
        high availability
After:  Maintain 14 backend services handling 40M daily requests;
        cut Sev-1 incidents from 6 a quarter to 1 by replacing
        cron-based retries with an idempotent queue consumer
```

The rewrite adds three things the original lacked: the scale (14 services, 40M requests), the change (six incidents to one), and the mechanism that produced the change. The mechanism is what makes the change believable, and it is where the interviewer will start.

### 6.2 Vagueness — no size, no period, no baseline

The bullet names a real activity and refuses to say how much of it there was. It reads as modesty and lands as absence.

```text
Before: Managed a team and delivered several successful projects
After:  Led a team of 9 engineers across 3 squads for 2 years,
        delivering the payments migration 5 weeks early with no
        customer-visible downtime
```

Three questions were unanswered and are now answered: how many, for how long, and how did it go. Note that "several successful projects" became one named project. One specific thing beats three unspecified ones, every time.

### 6.3 Self-assessment instead of instance

You cannot award yourself a quality. The reader discounts every adjective a candidate applies to themselves to approximately zero, and a citation-based model has nothing to quote.

```text
Before: Excellent problem solver with strong analytical skills
After:  Diagnosed a memory leak that had caused weekly production
        restarts for 8 months; traced it to an unbounded cache in a
        third-party SDK and shipped a bounded replacement in 3 days
```

The second version never claims to be a good problem solver. It does not need to: it is an instance of solving a problem, and the reader forms the judgement themselves, which is the only way a judgement is worth anything. The full inventory of self-assessment phrases to delete is in [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md).

### 6.4 Padding that survives because it sounds like work

The hardest category to delete, because every line in it is true. Attending ceremonies, collaborating cross-functionally, working in an agile environment, participating in code review, communicating with stakeholders — all real, all describing the ambient conditions of employment rather than anything you did.

```text
Before: Collaborated with cross-functional teams in an Agile
        environment to deliver features on time
After:  Negotiated a shared API contract with 3 consuming teams,
        unblocking a release that had slipped twice
```

**The test: would any competent holder of this title have written this line?** If yes, delete it. The space is worth more than the line is, because every padded bullet dilutes the density of the ones around it — and density is what a 17-second skim is measuring, whether or not the reader would put it that way.

## 7. The credibility constraint

This is where quantification goes wrong, and it goes wrong in the opposite direction from the one people expect.

**A number you cannot defend is worse than no number.** The vague bullet is weak. The indefensible number is disqualifying, and it takes the rest of the document down with it: once a reviewer establishes that one figure was decorative, every other figure on the page is re-read as decoration.

### 7.1 Do not invent precision

Decimal places you did not measure, year-over-year deltas you never tracked, and figures accurate to a single unit on a quantity nobody counts — these are exactly the fabrication signature that marks an invented statistic in an article, and reviewers who read hundreds of resumes recognise it without being able to name it.

<!-- kb:allow-number 27.3 reason="an invented figure shown as an example of false precision on a resume, not a claim about the world" -->
<!-- kb:allow-number 1,247,000 reason="an invented figure shown as an example of false precision on a resume, not a claim about the world" -->

| Instead of | Write | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Improved conversion by 27.3%" | "Improved conversion by roughly a quarter" | Unless the experiment reported a decimal, the decimal is fiction |
| "Saved $1,247,000 annually" | "Saved over $1M annually" | Round to the precision you can source |
| "Managed a $2M budget" when you approved spend within it | "Approved spend against a $2M budget" | The verb carries the claim; keep it true |
| "Increased team velocity by half" | "Cut mean cycle time from 11 days to 6" | Velocity is unit-free and unfalsifiable; cycle time is neither |

**Round down.** A defensible figure that understates costs you nothing and survives contact with the truth. An overstated one has to be walked back live, in front of the person deciding whether to hire you.

### 7.2 If you estimated, be able to show the estimate

Estimation is legitimate. Hiding that you estimated is not. Before a figure goes on the page, answer two questions in one sentence each:

1. **Where did this come from?** "The dashboard for Q3." "I counted the tickets." "Eight people times four hours a week, from the rota." Any of these is a good answer. "It felt like about a third" is not, until you have converted it into one of the above.
2. **What would make it wrong?** If the answer is "nothing I can think of", you have not understood your own number. Every real measurement has a boundary — a period it covers, a population it excludes, a confound it cannot rule out. Knowing yours is what makes you sound like the person who did the work.

Signalling the estimate in the bullet itself is free and buys enormous latitude: *approximately*, *roughly*, *around*, *over*. A hedged figure invites a conversation. A falsely exact one invites an audit.

Where no honest estimate is available at all, stop reaching for a number and switch instruments — scale, scope, frequency, duration, breadth and counterfactual are all evidence, and they are the subject of [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](../patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md).

### 7.3 Attribution: whose number is it

The team's outcome is not automatically yours. Two safe constructions and one unsafe one:

- **Safe:** name your part inside the team's outcome. "Owned the reconciliation service in a 12-person migration that cut settlement time from 3 days to 4 hours."
- **Safe:** claim influence explicitly. "Contributed to", "supported", "one of four engineers on" — modest phrasing with a real number beats a grand claim with a soft one.
- **Unsafe:** the bare possessive on a collective result. "Delivered a €4M revenue increase" when you built one feature in the funnel. It will not survive the first follow-up question, and the follow-up question is guaranteed, because that is the largest number on your page.

### 7.4 The probe, and the actual failure mode

Both readers probe, in different ways and for the same reason.

**A competent interviewer** picks the biggest number on the page and asks how it was measured. This is not an attempt to catch you; it is the cheapest available way to find out whether you think quantitatively about your own work. The answer they are listening for is a method, not a defence.

**An LLM screener** probes structurally rather than conversationally: it looks for the span that supports a criterion and reports what it found. Where a resume claims an outcome the surrounding text does not support — a scale with no system named, a saving with no mechanism — the criterion comes back undecided, and undecided is a quiet loss you never hear about.

**The failure mode is not being caught out on the number. It is being caught out on the fact that you do not know your own work.** A candidate who says "roughly a third, measured off the billing console across two quarters — it excludes the data-transfer line, which we never separated" has just demonstrated more competence than the figure itself ever could. A candidate who says "I think that was in the deck somewhere" has demonstrated the opposite, and no amount of formatting recovers it.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I need to pick between X-Y-Z, CAR and STAR" | There is nothing to pick. They are one formula — outcome, magnitude, method — differing only in ordering. Lead with the outcome and stop reading formula comparisons. |
| "Every bullet starts with 'Responsible for'" | You have written the job description you were given rather than the work you did. Any successor in the seat could have written the same page. |
| "I wrote 'extensive experience with Kotlin' so the model counts it" | A citation-based evaluator has nothing to quote in an adjective, so the honest verdict is "does not meet". Dates, systems and quantities are citable; qualities are not. |
| "I put the technology first because that's the keyword" | Retrieval matches the whole document; the human reads the first four words. Keeping the term mid-sentence satisfies both, and outcome-first satisfies the reader. |
| "I don't have numbers, my work isn't measurable" | Almost always false before you have looked. Six families of metric exist and most jobs touch three of them. If it survives an honest search, switch instruments rather than inventing one. |
| "I rounded my figure up to sound stronger" | You now have to defend it live. Round down: an understated figure that survives a follow-up is worth more than an impressive one that does not. |
| "I wrote 27.3% because precision looks rigorous" | False precision is the same fabrication signature on a resume as it is in an article, and experienced reviewers spot it without being able to name it. |
| "My bullet is four lines because the achievement is complex" | It is not read. A three-line bullet is a paragraph in disguise, and it is usually two achievements that should be two lines. |
| "I claimed the team's revenue number because I was on the team" | It is the largest number on your page, so it is the first thing probed. Name your part inside the team's outcome instead. |
| "I use the same five bullets for every role so the page looks balanced" | Uniformity tells the reader you have not decided what matters. Weight the recent role and prune the old ones to one line. |
