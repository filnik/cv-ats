# LinkedIn as a Parallel Channel

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global** | **ATS: linkedin (Recruiter and Easy Apply)**

**Read when:** recruiters never find you · what the headline should say · Open to Work and whether your employer sees it · endorsements · Easy Apply or the company site · whether the profile should just mirror the resume · whether posting helps

**Not this file:** [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](05-keywords-and-tailoring.md) — how to choose the terms in the first place, and how they behave inside a resume. This document is about the profile as a retrieval surface.

## TL;DR

- **Spend the first hour on the profile, not the resume.** LinkedIn is the channel recruiters source from most, and being sourced skips a queue that has roughly doubled in length while the teams reading it more than halved.
- **Optimise for the documented filters, not the opaque ranking.** Search *relevance* is proprietary and explicitly personalised per searcher; the Skills filter, the location filter and the spotlights are published, deterministic and yours to satisfy.
- **Every skill you want to be found for goes in the skills section explicitly** — it is the only surface LinkedIn documents as standardised. Then carry the same terms as prose through your headline, About and every position description, because those are documented extraction surfaces too.
- **Your profile location is a hard gate.** A profile pinned to the wrong country is the single most common structural reason a good candidate never appears in any result set, and the failure is silent.
- **Turn the private, recruiters-only Open to Work setting on and leave it on.** It costs nothing and feeds a first-class filter. The public photo frame is a separate decision, made by situation, not by rule.
- **Apply on the company site by default.** No reliable Easy Apply conversion data exists in either direction, but the structural arguments do not need numbers.

## 1. Why the profile now outranks the resume in expected value

A resume is a document you push into a queue. A profile is a record that sits in an index other people query. In 2026 those two things have very different odds attached to them.

> **Study (n=384 HR professionals directly involved in hiring, random sampling February to March 2025):** Jobscan's State of the Job Search found LinkedIn to be the leading sourcing channel — roughly three quarters of respondents source there — ahead of employee referrals and agencies at 60%, social platforms at 55%, job boards at 48% and their own ATS archive at 41%. Separately, 73.3% said they always or often evaluate a candidate's LinkedIn profile as part of assessment.

Two figures from the other side of the funnel explain why that matters more this year than last.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Greenhouse's benchmark across 6,000+ companies and 640M+ applications found applications per job rose 111% between 2022 and 2025, while recruiters per organisation fell 56%. Ashby's dataset across 100M+ applications reports applications per hire have tripled since 2021 to more than 300, and that a candidate is approximately 50% less likely to receive an interview than five years ago.

Put those together and the arithmetic is blunt. The inbound queue has roughly doubled per job and tripled per hire, and the number of humans available to read it has more than halved. Nothing you do to a resume changes your position in that queue. It only changes whether you survive being read, if you ever reach the front of it.

**Being sourced does not join the queue.** A recruiter running a search in Recruiter is constructing a shortlist from an index, not triaging an inbox. The candidates in that shortlist compete against twenty or thirty profiles, not three hundred applications. That asymmetry — not any claim about profile views — is the entire argument for spending an hour on the profile before spending another hour on the resume.

It is also why the profile is not a copy of the resume. The resume answers "should we advance this application?" The profile answers a prior question: "does this person exist in the result set at all?" Those are different jobs and they reward different work. The resume rewards **evidence**; the profile rewards **coverage** — being present in every filter a plausible recruiter would apply. Section 13 covers what the two documents must nonetheless agree on.

## 2. How ranking actually works, and where knowledge runs out

Be disciplined here, because this is where almost every LinkedIn guide starts inventing.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** LinkedIn's Recruiter help documentation states that relevance is determined by a proprietary algorithm that LinkedIn continuously improves, that results are tailored to the person searching, and that "the same search can look different for different people". The signals it names are the searcher's activity on LinkedIn, the profiles that match the query, patterns from other members who ran similar searches, and the searcher's past search history.

Read what that rules out. Ranking is **personalised per searcher**, which means there is no such thing as "your position in LinkedIn search". Two recruiters typing an identical Boolean string see different orders, and neither order is a property of your profile alone. It also means nobody outside LinkedIn can measure a per-field ranking weight, because there is no stable ranking to measure against.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** LinkedIn publishes a help page titled "Practices to avoid when optimizing your profile for LinkedIn search". Its advice is to use multiple keywords rather than repeating the same words or phrases, to keep the name fields to a first and last name with no extra information, and not to artificially inflate the Experience and Education fields. It states that the Trust and Safety team reviews and takes action on content that violates the User Agreement.

So the platform's own instruction is: include terms that accurately describe your expertise, and do not stuff. Over-optimising is not a clever exploit LinkedIn has failed to notice; it is a documented anti-pattern with an enforcement path attached.

**The consequence, stated plainly: any source that gives you a per-field ranking weight — "the headline carries three times the weight of the About section", "skills are 30% of the algorithm" — is inventing it. This document will not.**

What remains is better than it sounds, and it is the spine of everything below.

| | Ranking | Filters |
|---|---|---|
| Documented? | No — proprietary, and explicitly personalised | Yes — the sources and the behaviour are published |
| Deterministic? | No | Yes: you are in the result set or you are not |
| Under your control? | Marginally, and unmeasurably | Almost entirely |
| Failure mode | You appear lower | You do not appear, and nothing tells you |

**Optimise for the filters. Ignore the ranking.** A filter is binary: a recruiter selects `Kubernetes` plus `Berlin` and you are either in that set or absent from it. Absence is silent and total, and no amount of ranking luck recovers from it. The rest of this document is the list of filters worth satisfying, in descending order of what is actually known about each.

## 3. The Skills filter: the one documented mechanic worth building around

This is the highest-value verified thing in the whole subject, and almost nobody teaches it from the source.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** LinkedIn Recruiter's help documentation for the Skills filter names four sources it draws from. **Explicit skills:** skills listed in the member's skills section, "which are standardized". **Implicit skills:** skills extracted from text within any section of the profile, "such as the summary, position description, title, and headline". **Skill keywords on the profile:** skills mentioned anywhere on the profile outside the dedicated section, including summaries, experience descriptions, certificates and courses. **Resume skills:** skills mentioned somewhere on a resume the member has chosen to share through the platform. The same documentation notes that a keyword search for `SaaS` also returns members whose profile says "Software as a service" without using the acronym.

Five rules fall straight out of that paragraph. They are not opinions; they are restatements of the mechanism.

### 3.1 Put every skill you want to be found for in the skills section, explicitly

The skills section is the only one of the four sources LinkedIn describes as **standardised**. Standardised means your string is resolved to the same entity the recruiter's filter selects. Everywhere else, your term is extracted from free text and matched by inference.

That distinction is the reason the section exists at all. A skill that appears only inside a job description may be extracted. A skill in the skills section is registered. Treat the section as your declared, machine-readable claim set:

- **Fill it.** The section holds up to 50 entries, and a profile using six of them is throwing away the cheapest coverage available anywhere on the platform.
- **Pin the top skills to the ones you want to be hired for.** Ordering demonstrably matters to the human reading the profile; whether it matters to a machine is not published, so do not spend time on it beyond that.
- **Never list a skill you cannot defend.** Recruiters interview from this list, and it is the first place an interviewer probes. The same rule governs the resume skills block — see [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](05-keywords-and-tailoring.md).
- **Prune.** Fifty entries that include `Microsoft Word` and `Teamwork` dilute the human signal without adding a filter anyone runs.

### 3.2 Treat the headline, About, titles and every job description as extraction surfaces

The documentation names them individually: summary, position description, title, headline. This is the rule with the largest practical consequence, because it converts three sections most people write as decoration into machine-readable inventory.

**An Experience entry with an empty description is a wasted asset.** It contributes nothing to sources two and three, and there are usually five or six of them on a profile. Filling them is the single highest-yield hour available. Section 8 covers how.

### 3.3 You do not need acronym-plus-expansion here

On a resume, write `Kubernetes (K8s)` and `Search Engine Optimisation (SEO)`, because parsers and Boolean strings are literal and you cannot know which form the recruiter typed. That rule is in [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](05-keywords-and-tailoring.md) and it stands there.

On LinkedIn it is unnecessary. The platform documents alias normalisation with its own example: `SaaS` retrieves profiles saying "Software as a service". Writing both forms on a profile buys nothing and costs the readability that the anti-stuffing guidance asks for. Write the form a human would say out loud.

The exception is a genuinely ambiguous abbreviation whose expansions are different concepts rather than aliases — `PM` is not an alias of anything in particular. Write those out.

### 3.4 A shared resume is a fourth surface

Skills on a resume you have shared through the platform count toward the filter. This is one of the few genuine mechanical arguments in favour of ever using Easy Apply, and it is worth knowing before section 12 argues against it: a resume attached to a LinkedIn application is not only sent to the employer, it enters your own findability.

It also feeds a spotlight — see section 5. **Keep a current resume on file in the profile's job-application settings even if you never apply through LinkedIn.**

### 3.5 What the filter does not name

Endorsements. Connection count. Post frequency. Profile completeness badges. None of these appears in the documented Skills filter, and section 9 draws the consequences.

### 3.6 What the rest of the structured filters read

Skills is the filter worth the most attention, but it is not the only one, and each of the others is fed by a specific field you control. A recruiter builds a search by stacking three or four of these, and every one of them is an opportunity to be absent.

| Recruiter filter | Profile field that feeds it | What that means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Job titles (current, past, or either) | The title string on each position | Use the market-standard title. An invented internal title is a filter you never appear in, at any company. |
| Companies (current or past) | The employer entity on each position | Attach each role to the real company page, not a free-text string, so you appear when someone searches the employer. |
| Years of experience | Computed from position date ranges | Every role needs both months filled in. A missing end date or an undated role distorts the number a `5+ years` filter compares against. |
| Schools and degrees | The Education entries | Fill them even if you think the degree is irrelevant; some searches are still built on them. |
| Industry | The single industry field on your profile | It is one dropdown, most people set it once and never look again, and it silently scopes you out of industry-filtered searches. Check it. |
| Languages spoken | The Languages section | Cheap, under-filled, and decisive for multi-market roles. |
| Seniority | Inferred from titles and tenure | Another reason the title string has to be the conventional one. |

The pattern across the whole table is the same as section 4: **a blank field is not neutral, it is an exclusion.** The filter runs on what is there.

## 4. Location is a hard gate

More candidates disappear here than for any other single reason, and it is invisible when it happens.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** LinkedIn's Recruiter help for location search documents three location types — **Current** (candidates whose stated current location is the searched location, or who work for a company operating there), **Current or open to relocate**, and **Open to relocate only**. There is an implied 100-mile radius around any location selection, refinable with a postal-code filter. Workplace type changes the behaviour: an onsite search returns candidates residing within, or open to relocating within, that radius; a **remote** search does not use location at all; a hybrid search includes members open to relocating to the region or within the radius.

Four consequences:

1. **Set your profile location to the metro you are targeting, expressed the way the platform names it.** A profile located in a small town thirty miles outside a major city is usually fine — the radius covers it. A profile located in a country you are not targeting is not fine, and no amount of keyword work rescues it.
2. **Fill in the relocation preference if it is true.** It is what populates "Current or open to relocate", and a recruiter running that mode is explicitly looking for people like you. Leaving it blank excludes you from the one filter designed to include you.
3. **Remote roles are the exception that proves the gate.** For a remote-flagged search, location is not applied at all. If remote is your strategy, say so in the Open to Work preferences and in the headline — but understand that you are then competing in a global result set, which is the largest one that exists.
4. **Do not fabricate a location you cannot legally work from.** Work authorisation is a knockout question later in the process, and answering it truthfully after a fabricated location has done the introductions wastes a recruiter's time and burns your credibility. Being findable is not the same as being eligible.

The honest version of the trade-off: if you are genuinely relocating, set the target location and say so in the first two lines of the About section. If you are not, do not.

### 4.1 Profile language, for anyone searching across a border

LinkedIn lets you keep versions of your profile in more than one language, with one of them designated primary. It does not document how a secondary-language version interacts with the Skills filter, so do not build a strategy on it — assume the primary version is the one that counts.

**Write the primary profile in the language of the market you are targeting.** If you live in Milan and want a role with an international team, the primary profile is in English, because the skill terms a recruiter types are English and a profile that describes itself in Italian does not contain those strings. Keep an Italian secondary version if local employers matter to you, and let the primary one be the version that has to win.

Two things stay in the local convention regardless: the company's own legal name, written as it is registered, and any local certification that has no English equivalent. Both are searched as-is. Country-by-country document conventions are a separate subject — see [references/market-conventions.md](../references/market-conventions.md).

## 5. Spotlights: filters you can deliberately qualify for

Beyond keyword and location filters, Recruiter offers one-click **spotlights** that narrow a result set to candidates with a particular relationship to the searcher's company. They sit at the top of the results page, and a recruiter with three hundred matches will click one before reading anything.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** LinkedIn's Recruiter help documents the spotlights and how a candidate qualifies for each. **Open to work** — members who have indicated they are open to hearing from recruiters, including those sharing privately with Recruiter users only. **Active talent** — members who recently updated their profile, chose to share a resume with recruiters, or have been in their current role longer than the average tenure. **Have company connections** — candidates who are 1st-degree connections of employees at the searcher's organisation. **Interested in your company** — candidates who selected "I'm interested" in future roles there, or interacted with the company's content. **Rediscovered candidates** — people the team has previously interacted with who match the current search. **Internal candidates** — current employees in their role at least 6 months. **Applicants**, **Missed candidates** (uncontacted for over a week), **Candidates saved by** a recruiter or the AI agent, and **Verifications** — applicants carrying profile verifications such as workplace, school or government ID.

Four of those are things you can cause on purpose, which makes this the most under-used list in the whole subject:

| Spotlight | What you do about it |
|---|---|
| Open to work | Turn on the private setting. Section 10.1. |
| Active talent | Make a real profile edit before a search push, and keep a current resume on file. Both are documented qualifiers. |
| Have company connections | Connect with people who actually work at your target companies — not recruiters only, engineers and managers too. First-degree is the documented threshold. |
| Interested in your company | Follow the target company page and use the "I'm interested" control where it exists. It costs one click and puts you in a set the recruiter opens first. |
| Verifications | Complete identity and workplace verification. Section 11.1. |

None of this is a growth hack. It is the platform telling you, in its own documentation, exactly which sets it will let a recruiter filter down to — and four of those sets have an open door.

## 6. Headline

The headline is a documented extraction surface for the Skills filter *and* the string that appears in every search result row, every InMail thread and every comment you leave. That is the justification for taking it seriously. It is not an engagement statistic, and any guide quoting one for the headline is quoting something nobody has measured.

**The rules:**

1. **Front-load the target job title as a clean, quotable string.** The first thing in the headline is the title a recruiter would type. `Senior Backend Engineer` — not `Backend enthusiast`, not `Code wrangler`.
2. **Follow it with three to five hard skills**, separated by a pipe or a middle dot. Concrete nouns only: languages, platforms, domains, certifications.
3. **Front-load the first characters.** The field allows 220 characters and you should use most of them, but the mobile preview and search-result rows truncate early — assume roughly the first 70 characters are all that many readers see. Everything decisive goes before the first separator.
4. **Never make `seeking`, `aspiring`, `open to work`, `unemployed` or `looking for opportunities` the primary content.** This is the rule people resist and it is the most important one here. Those terms occupy the most visible text you own, and they appear in recruiters' Boolean NOT clauses — see [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md) for the exclusion vocabulary. Your job-search status already has a dedicated, filterable mechanism (section 10); the headline is not it.
5. **`Ex-BigCo` is weaker than it feels**, and section 11.2 explains why the platform is deliberately moving away from rewarding it.

```text
Weak:    Experienced professional | Open to new opportunities | Team player
Better:  Senior Backend Engineer — Kotlin, Go, Kubernetes | Payments & high-throughput APIs | Fintech → B2B SaaS
```

The second version contains one string a recruiter would search, four terms they would filter on, and a domain that tells a human whether to click. The first is invisible to every filter and says nothing to a person.

## 7. About

Same justification, same discipline: About is a named extraction surface, and it is also the only place on the profile where you speak in your own voice at length.

1. **The first two lines are all that shows before the fold** — roughly 300 characters on desktop, closer to 200 on mobile, before "see more". Lead with role, years, domain, and two or three signature outcomes. If a recruiter never expands the section, those lines must still have done the job.
2. **Write first-person prose.** Not because prose is better writing, though it is, but because this text is read twice by two very different readers: an extractor pulling skill nouns out of it, and a human deciding whether to spend an InMail credit. Prose satisfies both. A bare keyword list satisfies neither, and it is exactly what the anti-stuffing guidance in section 2 warns against.
3. **Use the space.** The field allows 2,600 characters. Three or four short paragraphs is the right shape: what you do and for whom, the two or three outcomes you would want quoted back at you, the domains and tools you know, and what you are looking for next.
4. **Put a plain-text skills paragraph near the end.** One readable, sentence-shaped list of tools and domains — not a wall of comma-separated tokens. It gives the extractor a dense, honest patch of nouns without turning the section into a keyword dump.
5. **Include an email address if you want to be contactable.** Most recruiters do not hold a Recruiter seat and cannot InMail you; an internal recruiter at a small company frequently cannot message a non-connection at all. A plain email address on its own line removes that barrier entirely, and it is the cheapest conversion improvement on the profile.

What not to do: do not paste the resume summary verbatim. The resume's top third is written for a reader who has already opened your application — see [guides/03-the-top-third.md](03-the-top-third.md). The About section is written for someone deciding whether you are worth approaching at all.

## 8. Experience: the most neglected surface on the platform

Every position on the profile is a documented extraction surface, and it contributes to two of the four Skills-filter sources at once — the position description and the title. Most profiles leave the descriptions empty. This is free coverage lying on the floor.

1. **Every position gets a description containing real skill nouns.** Not a mission statement. What you built, with what, at what scale. The field allows 2,000 characters per role; two or three sentences plus three or four bullets is plenty.
2. **Open each role with a one-line scope sentence** before the achievements: team size, stack, domain, scale. `Backend team of six, Kotlin and Postgres on AWS, payments for a consumer app with a few hundred thousand monthly actives.` One line, and it usually carries more filterable nouns than the four bullets under it.
3. **Reuse your resume bullets.** The profile is not required to differ from the resume, only to be complete. Copying the strongest bullets across is fine and fast. What is not fine is copying three of them and leaving four roles blank. Bullet construction itself is in [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](04-bullets-and-evidence.md).
4. **Use the market-standard job title, with any internal title in parentheses.** `Senior Software Engineer (Tech Lead II)`. The searchable string must be the one people search for — the same rule that governs the resume, for the same reason, described mechanically in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md).
5. **Old roles still count.** A skill you last used in 2019 is still an extraction source. Do not delete a role because it feels distant; trim its description instead.
6. **Every role carries dates.** Not for the parser — there is no parser here — but because a recruiter comparing your profile against your resume is checking exactly this. Section 13.

## 9. Endorsements: what is actually known, which is very little

Handle this with the same discipline as section 2, because it is the part of LinkedIn advice with the widest gap between confidence and evidence.

**Nothing is published about whether or how endorsements affect Recruiter ranking.** The documented Skills filter — quoted in full in section 3 — names four sources, and endorsement counts are not among them, nor are they described as a condition on any of the four. That is not proof they do nothing. It is the absence of any reason to believe they do something.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** Claims such as "profiles with 15+ endorsed skills get 22x more recruiter views, up from 17x in 2024" and "endorsements from Directors carry 3.4x the algorithmic weight" circulate widely. No dataset, no method and no publisher has been located for either. Both carry the fabrication signature described in [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md): a threshold, a multiplier with decimal precision, a year-over-year delta, and nobody's name on it.

**The defensible position, which is what you should act on:**

- Endorsements are a weak **human** credibility signal, not a demonstrated ranking factor. A recruiter glancing at a skill with a handful of endorsements reads "other people have seen this person do this". A skill with zero endorsements, on a profile where nothing has any, reads "abandoned account".
- A handful on your top few skills is worth having for that reason alone. It makes the profile look inhabited.
- **Chasing dozens is waste.** There is no documented threshold, no evidence of a ranking effect, and reciprocal endorsement trading is visible to anyone who opens both profiles.
- **Verified skill assessments are a stronger credibility signal per minute spent.** They are a platform-administered test with a pass badge, not a click from a colleague.
- Written recommendations are a separate object: they are prose on your profile, and plausibly fall within "any section of the profile", though LinkedIn does not enumerate them. Get them for the human reader — a named former manager describing your work outperforms any number of one-click endorsements — and treat any extraction benefit as an unverifiable bonus.

## 10. Open to Work: two settings, two different decisions

These get conflated constantly. They are separate switches with separate evidence behind them.

### 10.1 The private setting: always on

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** The recruiters-only mode lets members share their career interests privately with Recruiter users who are not affiliated with their current or related companies. Candidates using it appear in the **Open to work** spotlight in Recruiter search results, but the `#OPENTOWORK` photo frame is not displayed. LinkedIn states that it takes steps to prevent Recruiter users at your current employer from seeing the status, while explicitly not guaranteeing complete privacy.

The spotlight is a first-class filter in the recruiter's interface — one click, at the top of the results, narrowing to people who have raised their hand. Being outside it means competing for attention against candidates who have.

**Turn it on and leave it on.** The only real cost is the unguaranteed part of the privacy promise, which matters if your employer runs Recruiter seats and you sit in a small, visible team. That is a judgement about your specific employer, not a general reason to leave the setting off.

While you are in there, **fill in the preferences properly** — target titles, locations, start date, workplace type, full-time or contract. These feed filters. A blank preference is a filter you are excluded from, exactly as in section 4.

### 10.2 The public photo frame: by situation

> **Vendor-documented (2020):** LinkedIn's own blog reported that members using the public `#OpenToWork` photo frame received on average 40% more InMails from recruiters, measured across the more than three million members who had used it. This is the platform's own measurement of its own product, which is the best possible source for the claim — and it is six years old; a widely circulated 2026 restatement of it, with a larger sample and a fuller metric set, is fabricated and appears on no LinkedIn property.

The counter-argument deserves an honest hearing rather than dismissal. Some senior recruiters and executive search consultants report reading the public frame as a negative signal at executive level — an availability signal that weakens negotiating position, or a hint of a non-voluntary exit. **That is opinion, not data.** It is sincerely held by experienced people, it has no measurement behind it, and it is contradicted by the only measurement that exists.

So recommend by situation rather than by rule:

| Situation | Public frame |
|---|---|
| Unemployed, individual-contributor or mid-level role | **On.** The measured benefit is real and the reputational cost is close to zero. |
| Employed, discreet search | **Off.** Private setting only. The frame is visible to your employer and to everyone else. |
| Employed, senior or executive, brand-sensitive market | **Off.** Not because the frame is harmful, but because the private setting captures nearly all of the benefit without the visible signal. |
| Unemployed at executive level | **On, then off.** Turn it on to prime the pipeline; turn it off once inbound conversations are running. |
| Freelance or contract, permanently available | **On.** Availability is the product. |

The general principle: the frame's benefit is measured and its cost is contested. Where the cost is plausibly zero, take the measured benefit.

## 11. Verification, and what changed in 2025 and 2026

### 11.1 Verify your identity

Free identity verification is now available in a large and growing number of countries, done in the mobile app in a few minutes — a government ID or NFC passport scan plus a selfie, through LinkedIn's verification partners. Workplace verification through a work email address or a corporate directory is separate, and equally free.

**Do it.** The justification is not any engagement claim — several circulate and none is verifiable. It is two things that are documented.

The first is the market it sits in:

> **Vendor-documented (2025):** Greenhouse's workforce research reports that 22% of active job seekers admit to using bots to apply automatically, rising to 31% among Gen Z, and cites a Gartner projection that by 2028, 25% of job applicants will be fake.

The second is mechanical: **Verifications is one of the documented spotlights** in section 5. A verified profile is not merely more trustworthy in the abstract; it is a set a recruiter can filter down to in one click. In a market where a quarter of applicants may be synthetic within two years, five minutes once is an obvious trade.

### 11.2 The shift away from pedigree

Two product changes matter to how a profile should be written.

The first: LinkedIn's recruiting AI agent reached general availability and expanded globally during 2025, moving the product's centre of gravity from *search* to *agent*. A recruiter now describes a role in natural language, and the system builds and refines the sourcing strategy rather than executing a Boolean string the recruiter typed.

The second follows from it: AI-assisted search recommends candidates on **skills, experience and fit** rather than on previous employers and degrees. That is a documented, deliberate move away from pedigree.

**What this changes for you, concretely:**

- **Good news for career changers.** A system reasoning over demonstrated skills does not need you to have held the title before. The transferable-skills argument a human recruiter skims past is exactly what this layer is built to find. See [patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md](../patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md) for the narrative side of that.
- **Bad news for profiles built on brand names.** A profile whose evidence is `Ex-BigCo` with empty position descriptions gives a skills-first agent nothing to reason over. The brand still helps a human; it is increasingly inert to the layer that decides which humans see you at all.
- **It rewards complete prose over keyword lists.** A model reading your Experience descriptions as text extracts more from three well-written sentences than from thirty comma-separated tokens. That is the same advice the anti-stuffing guidance gives, which is a rare and welcome alignment.
- **Do not put prompt injections in your About section.** Hidden instructions aimed at an AI screener are detectable, are documented as self-defeating once more than a few candidates use them, and are covered in full in [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md). On LinkedIn they are additionally a User Agreement problem, which the resume version is not.

## 12. Easy Apply versus the company site

Start with what is not known, because the internet is confident about this and has no basis to be.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** The much-quoted claim that Easy Apply converts at 1 to 4% against 8.7% for direct applications has no published methodology from any source. LinkedIn publishes no Easy Apply conversion data. Neither does any ATS vendor. Every circulating number for this comparison is untraceable, including the ones that favour applying directly.

The structural argument does not need numbers, and it is strong:

1. **One click removes the friction that filters out low-intent applicants.** Friction is a sorting mechanism that works in your favour when you are a serious candidate. Remove it and the pool is maximal and maximally undifferentiated — which is precisely the queue described in section 1.
2. **Knockout questions are frequently absent.** This sounds like an advantage and is not. Where the employer's real form asks about work authorisation, notice period or a required certification, answering those questions is how you *demonstrate that you meet them*. A one-click application that never asked arrives with those fields blank, and blank is not the same as qualified.
3. **Custom fields and cover letters are unavailable or truncated.** Anything the employer specifically asked for, you cannot supply. Where a letter is worth writing at all, see [guides/06-cover-letters.md](06-cover-letters.md).
4. **Some teams never open the platform's applicant list.** Where the job was posted by an agency, syndicated automatically, or posted by a company whose recruiters live in their own ATS all day, the LinkedIn inbox is a second screen nobody has open.
5. **The company site puts your file through the employer's actual parser, into the system of record the recruiter works in daily.** That is where the search filters in [guides/01-how-screening-actually-works.md](01-how-screening-actually-works.md) get run, and where a well-parsed record earns you retrieval. [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md) is what makes that parse succeed.

**The recommendation:**

- **Company site by default.** Always, for any role you actually want.
- **One-click for a deliberate wide net only** — a volume day, a speculative sweep, roles you would take but are not targeting.
- **Never both for the same role.** Duplicate records in an ATS create merge work for the recruiter and read as scattergun. Pick one.
- **The highest-return move is neither.** After applying on the company site, find the recruiter or the hiring manager and send one short message referencing the requisition by name. That turns an anonymous row in a queue into a person with a face, which is the same mechanism that makes sourcing work — you are manufacturing the introduction the index would otherwise have had to find for you.

## 13. The profile and the resume must agree

Nearly three quarters of the recruiters in the Jobscan survey always or often open the profile as part of assessment, which means the two documents are read side by side more often than not. Any disagreement between them is a question you will be asked, and some of those questions you cannot win.

**The rule: the profile is the superset, the resume is the tailored subset. The facts are identical; the emphasis differs.**

| Must match exactly | May differ |
|---|---|
| Employer names | Which achievements are shown |
| Job titles, including the market-standard rendering | The order and weighting of skills |
| Start and end months | The length of each role's description |
| Education institutions and degrees | Tone and person — the resume is impersonal, the profile is first-person |

Three specific traps:

1. **A grander title on one than the other.** If the profile says `Head of Engineering` and the resume says `Senior Engineer`, one of them is inflated and the recruiter cannot tell which. Fix the title everywhere, using the market-standard form with the internal one in parentheses.
2. **A gap that is visible on one and papered over on the other.** Handle the gap the same way in both places; the strategy for that is in [patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md](../patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md).
3. **A stale profile.** A profile still showing a job you left eight months ago contradicts a resume that ends it in December. The profile is the version a reference-checker, a hiring manager and your future colleagues can all read without asking you — keep it current first.

Keeping *two resume versions* in sync is a different problem with its own treatment: [patterns/two-document-strategy.md](../patterns/two-document-strategy.md).

## 14. Activity, connections, and the only feedback loop you have

Three questions that come up constantly, answered at the confidence the evidence supports.

**Does posting and commenting help you get found?** Not through search, as far as anything published shows. Posting frequency appears in none of the documented filters, and the ranking is personalised and opaque. What posting does is put you in front of humans — a recruiter who already follows you, a former colleague who forwards a role, a hiring manager who recognises your name in a result set. That is a real channel and a slow one. Treat it as networking, not as retrieval optimisation, and do not let it displace section 16.

**Do connections matter?** Mechanically, yes, in one documented way: the **Have company connections** spotlight in section 5 selects candidates who are 1st-degree connections of employees at the searching organisation. Connecting with people who actually work at your target companies puts you in that set. It also determines whether a recruiter without a paid seat can message you at all. Connect with practitioners in your field and at your targets — not with everyone, and not only with recruiters.

**How do you know if any of this worked?** LinkedIn shows profile views and *search appearances*, including the job titles and companies of the people whose searches you appeared in. This is the only feedback loop candidates have, and it is weak: sampled, aggregated, delayed, and silent about the searches you did **not** appear in — which are the ones you actually care about. Read it for one thing only: whether the titles and companies appearing there are the ones you are targeting. If a backend engineer's search appearances are dominated by IT support recruiters, the headline and skills section are describing the wrong person. That signal is worth acting on. Nothing finer-grained in that panel is.

## 15. The human half: appearing is not the same as being contacted

Everything above gets you into the result set. What happens next is a person deciding whether to spend an InMail credit on you, and it is worth being explicit that **nobody has measured this.** No study of recruiter click behaviour inside Recruiter has been published, and the vendor does not release one. What follows is mechanism plus practitioner report, offered as a default rather than as evidence.

A search result row shows a photo, a name, the headline, the current position and the location. That is the whole first impression, and three of the five are fields covered above.

- **Have a photo.** This is the one place where the resume advice and the LinkedIn advice genuinely diverge. On a resume, a photograph is a market-specific question with a real and asymmetric evidence base — see [references/market-conventions.md](../references/market-conventions.md). On LinkedIn it is not a question: a profile without a photo reads as abandoned or fake in a market where a quarter of applicants may soon be synthetic, and the anonymisation argument does not apply to a profile that is public by design.
- **The headline is doing double duty.** It is a Skills-filter surface (section 3.2) and it is one of the five things in the row. That is why section 6 asks for a title plus concrete nouns rather than a slogan: the same string has to satisfy a filter and persuade a person.
- **Recency is visible.** A profile whose last role ended two years ago, or whose About section still describes a job you have left, reads as stale to a human — and a recent, genuine update is a documented qualifier for the Active talent spotlight (section 5). Both reasons point the same way.
- **Mutual connections are shown.** A shared connection converts a cold approach into a warmer one, and at the search level it is the Have company connections spotlight. This is the one place where networking and filtering are the same action.
- **Make the first two lines of About answer "why this person".** If a recruiter clicks through, that is what they read before deciding to write. Section 7.

None of this is a substitute for sections 3 through 5. You cannot be persuasive inside a result set you are not in.

## 16. The one-hour pass, in order of expected value

If you do nothing else, do these, in this order. The ordering is by how much documented mechanism each satisfies per minute spent.

1. **Location.** Correct city and country, relocation preference set if true. Two minutes, and it is a hard gate (section 4).
2. **Private Open to Work on, preferences filled in.** Three minutes, feeds a first-class spotlight (section 10.1).
3. **Skills section filled deliberately** — every term you want to be found for, pruned of filler, top skills pinned. Fifteen minutes, and it is the only standardised surface (section 3.1).
4. **Every Experience entry gets a description**, each opening with a one-line scope sentence. Twenty minutes, and it is the largest untouched extraction surface most profiles have (section 8).
5. **Headline rewritten**: target title first, three to five hard skills, no `seeking` (section 6).
6. **About rewritten**: two lines above the fold, prose, skills paragraph near the end, email address (section 7).
7. **Identity verification**, and a current resume left on file. Five minutes, once, and it qualifies you for two spotlights (sections 11.1 and 3.4).
8. **Follow your target companies and connect with a handful of people inside them.** Ten minutes, two more spotlights (section 5).

Endorsements are not on this list. That is deliberate.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "My LinkedIn is just my resume pasted in" | The resume answers whether to advance you. The profile answers whether you appear at all. Coverage across the documented surfaces — skills section, headline, About, every position description — is what the profile is for. |
| "I put Open to Work in my headline so recruiters know" | You spent your most visible, most searched string on a term that sits in recruiters' exclusion clauses, and duplicated a status that already has a dedicated, filterable mechanism. |
| "I'm getting no recruiter messages even though my profile is detailed" | Check the location first. A profile pinned to the wrong metro or country is absent from every geographic filter, and the failure is completely silent. |
| "I left the Experience descriptions blank because it's all on my resume" | Position descriptions are one of the four documented sources of the Skills filter. Blank descriptions are the largest single piece of unclaimed coverage on most profiles. |
| "I'm collecting endorsements to rank higher" | Endorsement counts appear nowhere in the documented Skills filter, and every specific multiplier attached to them is folklore with no dataset. A handful looks inhabited; dozens is time you could have spent on section 8. |
| "I wrote Kubernetes (K8s) everywhere, like on my resume" | LinkedIn documents alias and acronym normalisation with its own `SaaS` example. On the profile it buys nothing and costs the readability the platform's own anti-stuffing guidance asks for. |
| "I repeated my key skill twenty times so I'd rank for it" | LinkedIn's own guidance is to use multiple keywords rather than repeat one, and profile content is subject to Trust and Safety review. Over-optimisation is a documented anti-pattern, not an undiscovered exploit. |
| "I post every day, so recruiters must be finding me" | Posting appears in none of the documented filters. It reaches humans, slowly. It does not put you in a result set. |
| "I use Easy Apply because it's faster and I can do more of them" | You joined the least differentiated pool that exists, skipped the knockout questions that would have shown you qualify, and may have landed in an inbox the team never opens. |
| "I applied on LinkedIn and on the company site to double my chances" | Duplicate candidate records create merge work and read as scattergun. One application, on the company site, plus a direct message to the recruiter. |
| "I won't turn on the frame because a recruiter told me it looks desperate" | That is opinion, sincerely held and unmeasured, against LinkedIn's own measurement of the members who used it. Decide by situation, not by the anecdote. |
| "My profile says Head of Engineering and my resume says Senior Engineer" | Both documents get read side by side. One of them is inflated and the reader cannot tell which, so they assume the worse case. |
