# Cover Letters

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global, with a DACH and continental-European note** | **ATS: workday, greenhouse — for what they do *not* do with the file**

**Read when:** is it worth writing one · the field says optional · explaining a gap or a missing requirement · what goes in the first line · reusing one letter · will it read as AI-written

**Not this file:** [patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md](../patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md) — if the question is what the *resume* should say about a gap, a career change or a stack of short contracts, that is the file. This one covers only what the letter says.

## TL;DR

- **No ATS parses or scores a cover letter in any way that affects your odds.** It cannot get you retrieved and it cannot get you auto-rejected. Its entire life is with a human, and that is the only thing that should shape it.
- **The screener skips it; the decision-maker reads it.** That makes it a low-probability, high-payoff instrument: usually wasted, occasionally the reason you got the interview.
- **Never send a generic one.** The only experiment that exists measured 10.7% callbacks with no letter, 12.5% generic and 16.4% tailored — and it ran before AI could write a generic letter in four seconds. In 2026 the generic arm is worth zero or less.
- **Send one when** there is a field for it (including an optional one), the company is small, the role is senior, you have a referral or a real connection, or your resume raises a question it does not answer. Otherwise do not.
- **Four paragraphs, 250 to 350 words, one page.** Opening, one proof story with a number, why this company, close. The third paragraph is the entire effect: if you can swap the company name and it still reads sensibly, it is not a good letter.
- **The gap paragraph is the one thing a letter does that a resume cannot.** Name the gap in one sentence, spend three on what you did adjacent to it, and never apologise.
- **AI as an editor is accepted; AI as a ghostwriter is rejected.** The tell is not prose style — it is the absence of anything only you could have written.

## 1. The verdict: dead as a screening artefact, alive as a persuasion artefact

Almost every argument about cover letters is really two arguments wearing one name. One is *does a machine care* and the other is *does a person care*. The answers are different, and separating them dissolves most of the disagreement in the source material.

### 1.1 The machine does not care, and the mechanism is boring

There is no cover-letter field in the structured record that represents you inside an ATS. The parser's job is to turn a resume into name, contact block, employers with titles and dates, degrees and skills. A letter contributes to none of those.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Workday's administrator documentation for resume parsing describes populating candidate fields from an attached resume, warns that results vary with format and word order, and names Languages and Skills as fields it does **not** auto-fill. Cover letters do not appear in the document at all.

That absence is the whole story. Compare it with what recruiters actually query on:

> **Study (n=384 HR professionals directly involved in hiring, random sampling via Zoho Surveys, February to March 2025):** the fields recruiters filter on are skills 76.4%, educational background 59.7%, job titles 55.3%, certifications or licences 50.6%, years of experience 44.0% and location 43.4%, with more than 99.7% using filters at all.

Every one of those is a structured field extracted from a resume or typed into a form. None of them can be populated by a letter. A cover letter therefore cannot make you appear in a search result you would otherwise have missed, which is the mechanism by which a resume actually earns attention — see [guides/01-how-screening-actually-works.md](01-how-screening-actually-works.md).

It equally cannot get you rejected by software.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Greenhouse's auto-reject rules can act only on custom job-post questions of type Yes/No, single-select or multi-select, combined with AND across up to 5 questions and OR within a single question's options. They cannot fire on free text, on an attachment, or on a match score.

Auto-rejection acts on a knockout question you answered in a form. A letter is not a form field. **You cannot fail a machine with a cover letter, and you cannot pass one with it either.**

The practical consequence is that everything the resume-optimisation industry sells about cover letters — keyword density, ATS-friendly formatting, match scores — is solving a problem that does not exist. Do not put keywords in a letter to feed a parser. Put them there if they are true and they help a person, or leave them out.

### 1.2 The person sometimes cares a great deal

The letter's whole value is that a human might read it, and whether they do depends entirely on *which* human.

**The screener skips it.** A recruiter clearing a queue is doing a retrieval task, and the retrieval task runs on the resume.

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, covertly timed via Microsoft Clarity, resumes presented as images, 2023):** Jan Tegze measured 17 seconds on a short one-page resume, 27 seconds mid-length and 46 seconds long. Fastest observed 12 seconds; slowest 2 minutes 27 seconds.

Forty-six seconds is the budget for the *resume*. Nobody spending it is also reading three hundred words of prose. And the volume behind that number has got worse, not better:

> **Study (6,000+ companies, 640 million-plus applications, 2022 to 2025):** Greenhouse benchmark data shows applications per job rising from 116 to 244 while applications per recruiter per year rose from 146 to 746 and recruiters per organisation fell from 10.43 to 4.62.

**The decision-maker reads it.** The hiring manager choosing between four finalists is doing a different task: not filtering, but deciding. At that point the resumes are broadly interchangeable — they all cleared the bar — and the letter is the only document in the pile that says anything about judgement, motivation or fit.

This split is the one finding in the practitioner survey material that is both believable and useful, and it is worth stating carefully because the surveys themselves are not trustworthy.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** the widely circulated percentages for "how many recruiters read cover letters" range across a wide band depending on which survey you pick, and the surveys are overwhelmingly run by companies that sell cover-letter tooling or resume services. No instrument, sample frame or response rate is published for most of them. The numbers are not reproduced in this knowledge base. The *qualitative* split they converge on — first-pass screeners skip, hiring managers and small-company founders read — is corroborated by the structural argument above and is safe to act on.

**Therefore:** a cover letter is a lottery ticket with a large prize and a real price. The price is thirty to sixty minutes of your attention, which is the scarcest thing you have during a search. The strategy follows mechanically: **never buy a ticket that cannot win (a generic letter), and only buy one when the odds of a read are plausibly high.** Section 3 is the list of those cases.

## 2. The evidence, stated honestly

There is one randomised field experiment on cover letters. It is the only one located anywhere, and this knowledge base's rule is that a claim carries its weaknesses in the same breath as its result.

> **Study (7,287 applications to real job openings on ZipRecruiter, Glassdoor and Indeed; three-arm field experiment, 15 July 2019 to 10 January 2020, callbacks tracked over 30 days):** ResumeGo measured callback rates of 10.7% with no cover letter, 12.5% with a generic one and 16.4% with a tailored one — a 53% lift for tailored over none.

Now the limitations, all of which matter:

| Weakness | Why it changes how you read the number |
|---|---|
| **Vendor-run** | ResumeGo sells resume and cover-letter writing. A null result would have been commercially inconvenient. |
| **Unreplicated** | No independent group has repeated it. A single field experiment is a hypothesis with good manners. |
| **Per-arm sample sizes unpublished** | Without them you cannot compute a confidence interval, so the gap between the generic and tailored arms cannot be weighed. |
| **Fictitious applications to real employers** | Real recruiters spent real time on candidates who did not exist. That is an ethics problem, and it is also why the design is unlikely to clear peer review or be replicated by an academic group. |
| **It predates the AI flood** | Fielded in 2019 and 2020. Both the volume of applications and the cost of producing a plausible letter have changed since. |

That last point does more than add uncertainty — it kills one of the three arms outright.

**In 2020 a generic cover letter signalled effort.** It took twenty minutes to write and it proved someone had bothered. **In 2026 it signals the opposite**, because a model produces one in seconds and reviewers know it. The generic arm's small lift over nothing was measuring an effort signal that no longer transmits.

> **Study (Greenhouse 2025 workforce research, sample not disclosed, citing a Gartner projection):** 22% of active job seekers admit using bots to apply automatically, rising to 31% among Gen Z, and Gartner projects that by 2028, 25% of job applicants will be fake. This is vendor research from a company selling detection tooling: treat the direction as real and the magnitude as soft.

**What the evidence will support:** *tailored beats nothing.* The direction is credible on both experimental and mechanistic grounds.

**What it will not support:** the size of the effect, the value of a generic letter today, or any claim about cover letters in a specific market or seniority band. Treat 53% as a ceiling produced under favourable conditions by an interested party, not as a forecast for your next application.

The claim-by-claim audit of this and every other contested figure in the field is [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md); this document does not repeat it.

## 3. When to send one, and when not to

Given a low read probability and a real cost, the whole game is rationing. With more than 300 applications per hire in the current market and a global median application-to-interview rate of 3.6 to 4.7%, you cannot tailor a letter to every posting and you should not try. Spend them where the read probability is highest.

### 3.1 Send one

| Case | Why the odds of a read are high |
|---|---|
| **There is a field for it, required or optional** | An optional field is itself a filter. Someone configured it deliberately, which means someone intends to look. Leaving it empty is a visible choice, not a neutral one. |
| **The company is small** | Below roughly fifty people the person reading applications is usually the person who will manage you. There is no screening layer to skip it. |
| **The role is senior** | Judgement, scope and reasons are the things being assessed at senior level, and a resume states outcomes rather than reasons. |
| **Your resume raises a question it does not answer** | A gap, a career change, a relocation, overqualification, a stated requirement you do not meet. Section 5. |
| **You have a specific, verifiable connection** | A referral, genuine use of the product, or a real opinion about a problem the company has stated publicly. Name the referrer in the first paragraph. |
| **The organisation is mission-driven** | Non-profits, public bodies, research institutes, values-forward startups. Motivation is part of the rubric rather than an ornament on it. |
| **The market expects one** | In DACH the `Anschreiben` is the document the reader opens first. See [references/market-conventions.md](../references/market-conventions.md). |

On the referral case, note that the letter is the weakest part of that combination:

> **Study (54 million-plus applications across 93,000 jobs, January 2021 to March 2026, ATS platform telemetry):** Ashby reports referred candidates passing the initial screen at 52% against 35% for all candidates.

If you have a referral, the letter documents it; the referral itself is doing the work. Do not spend an hour on the letter and five minutes on the referral.

### 3.2 Do not send one

| Case | Reason |
|---|---|
| **It is not requested and there is no field** | Emailing an unsolicited letter into a portal-driven process reaches nobody. There is no route for it. |
| **A high-volume one-click application** | Easy Apply and its equivalents. The read probability is close to zero and the field is a formality. |
| **You would write a generic one** | This is the important row. A generic letter is barely better than nothing on 2020 evidence and plausibly worse than nothing today, because it spends the goodwill of the read on text that proves you did not look at the company. |

**The rule that falls out of this:** *if you do not have thirty minutes and a specific reason, do not attach a letter.* An empty optional field costs you a small signal. A generic letter costs you the one read you were going to get.

### 3.3 What a letter cannot fix

Being clear about the ceiling stops you spending a search's worth of evenings on the wrong artefact.

- **A knockout question.** If the form asks whether you hold the right to work in the country and you answer no, a configured rule can reject you before any human opens anything. The letter is downstream of that decision and cannot argue with it.
- **A location filter.** Location is one of the fields recruiters filter on, and the filter runs on a structured field, not on your explanation of why the commute is fine.
- **A resume nobody can find.** A parse failure does not reject you; it leaves you in the database as a record that never appears in a search. A brilliant letter attached to an unsearchable record is a brilliant letter nobody opens. See [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md).
- **A channel problem.** At current volumes a cold applicant needs a great many targeted applications per offer. If nothing is converting, the bottleneck is more often the channel than the document, and a referral moves the number far more than any paragraph — see section 3.1.

**Fix those first.** The letter is the last few percent, and it is only worth writing once the rest of the application is capable of reaching a person.

## 4. The structure

Four short paragraphs, 250 to 350 words, one page, never two. The full skeleton with the slots marked is in [`../templates/cover-letter.md`](../templates/cover-letter.md) and is not duplicated here; what follows is why each paragraph is shaped the way it is.

### 4.1 Opening — name the role and the one concrete thing

Two sentences. State the exact role title as posted, then the single most concrete reason you are a fit. Nothing else.

```text
Weak:   I am writing to express my strong interest in the Senior Backend Engineer
        position at Acme. I believe my background makes me an excellent candidate.

Strong: I am applying for the Senior Backend Engineer role (req 4471). I spent the
        last two years cutting p99 latency on a payments API from 840ms to 120ms
        while it went from 3,000 to 40,000 requests per minute.
```

The weak version contains no information. It is also the single most-flagged opening in 2026, listed under the generated-text group in [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md), because it is what a model writes when it has no facts to work with.

**Rules:** name the role exactly as posted, including a requisition number where one exists — some companies run several similar openings and misidentifying which one you want is disqualifying on its own. Where you have a referral, the referrer's name goes in the first sentence, because that is the sentence with the highest probability of being read.

### 4.2 Proof — one story, not a summary of your resume

Four to six sentences. Take the **hardest requirement in the posting** and show one instance of you doing that specific thing, with a number and the constraint that made it hard.

The most common failure here is a paragraph that paraphrases the resume. The reader already has the resume, attached to the same application, and it is better at being a resume than your prose is. A paragraph that adds no facts is a paragraph that wasted the read.

What a story has that a resume bullet does not:

- **The constraint.** What made it hard — a deadline, a headcount cap, a regulator, a legacy system nobody would let you replace.
- **The trade-off.** What you gave up to get the result. This is the strongest single element available to you, because a model with no access to your history cannot invent one that survives an interview question about it.
- **The outcome, with a number.** Where you have none, [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](../patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md) covers the legitimate substitutes — scale, frequency, breadth, counterfactual.

Pick the requirement the posting spends the most words on, or the one listed first. If the posting says "must be comfortable owning a service end to end in a team of three", the paragraph is about the time you did exactly that, not about your general enthusiasm for ownership.

### 4.3 Fit — the paragraph that is the entire effect

Two to four sentences on why **this** company. This is where the tailoring lives, and tailoring is where the whole measured effect came from.

```text
Weak:   I am excited by Acme's mission and its innovative culture, and I would love
        to contribute to such a fast-growing team.

Strong: Your engineering blog post on moving from per-tenant databases to a shared
        schema with row-level security describes the migration I ran in 2024, down
        to the same problem with connection-pool exhaustion at the pgbouncer layer.
        I have opinions about how you handled the backfill.
```

**The swap test.** Replace the company name with a direct competitor's. If the paragraph still reads sensibly, it is not a fit paragraph — it is filler, and it reads as filler. This test is a practitioner heuristic rather than a measured finding; no study validates it. It earns its place because it is the only check in this document that takes ten seconds and is impossible to fudge.

Legitimate material for this paragraph: a specific product decision, an engineering or design post they published, a change in their market, a named team, a public problem they have described, or genuine first-hand use of the thing they make. Illegitimate material: their stated values recited back to them, the size of their funding round, and the word "innovative".

Company-level research — reading a firm's published rubric and values off its public material, and how far that can be taken before it becomes mimicry — is [patterns/company-tailoring.md](../patterns/company-tailoring.md).

### 4.4 Close — direct, no grovelling

Two sentences. Name the one thing you would want to talk about, and state your availability or your notice period if it is relevant. Then stop.

```text
Weak:   Thank you so much for taking the time to consider my application. I would be
        thrilled to have the opportunity to discuss how I can add value to your team.

Strong: I would like to talk about how you are handling the backfill window, since I
        got that wrong the first time and would do it differently now. I am available
        from October and can start with four weeks' notice.
```

Do not thank the reader for their time in a sentence of its own. Do not say you would be thrilled, honoured or delighted. An admission of something you got wrong, as in the second version, is disproportionately effective and almost nobody does it: it is unfakeable, it demonstrates the reflection the role actually requires, and it gives the interviewer a question they want to ask.

### 4.5 Where the material for paragraph three comes from

Tailoring is the entire measured effect, so it is worth having a routine rather than staring at the company's home page hoping for inspiration. Twenty minutes, in this order, and stop at the first thing that gives you a real sentence:

1. **Their engineering, design or product blog.** The highest-yield source by a wide margin, because a post is a decision someone defended in public. You are looking for one you have an opinion about, not one you can compliment.
2. **The posting itself, read twice.** The requirement listed first and the requirement described at greatest length are the two that matter. If the posting mentions a specific system, tool or migration, that is the company telling you what the job is really about.
3. **Public repositories, changelogs and release notes.** Specific, dated, and impossible to fabricate a reference to.
4. **The product, used for fifteen minutes.** Only if you can say something concrete afterwards. "I signed up and it is great" is not concrete; "your onboarding asks for the tax ID before the company name, which is backwards for an Italian sole trader" is.
5. **Conference talks and podcast appearances by the team**, and the team page itself if it names who you would work with.
6. **Funding rounds, headcount and press releases.** Last, and mostly useless. Everyone mentions the funding round; it is the "innovative" of research.

Two constraints on all of it. **Do not flatter** — an observation is worth more than a compliment, and a mild disagreement stated respectfully is worth more than either, because it is evidence that you read the thing rather than skimmed it. And **do not fabricate familiarity**; claiming to be a long-time user of a product you installed this morning collapses in the first interview question about it.

Where you find nothing at all — a company with no public engineering presence, a generic posting, a recruiter-fronted role — that is itself a signal. It usually means the read probability is low, and section 3.2 applies.

The broader version of this research, aimed at what it legitimately changes in the resume, is [patterns/company-tailoring.md](../patterns/company-tailoring.md).

## 5. The gap or the missing requirement

This is the technique most worth learning here, because it is the one case where a letter genuinely outperforms a resume. A resume is a record; it states dates and facts and has no room to explain them. The reader who notices something odd in the dates has exactly two options — form a theory, or move on — and both of those go badly for you.

**The pattern is one sentence naming it, then three sentences on everything adjacent.**

Name it plainly, once, in the reader's own terms, without apologising and without a euphemism. Then spend the rest of the paragraph on what you did that is adjacent to the thing you are missing. That ratio is the whole technique: one part acknowledgement, three parts evidence. Stated that way it reads as confidence rather than concealment, and — more importantly — it **pre-empts the objection instead of leaving it to be discovered.** An objection the reader raises for themselves is a rejection. An objection you raised and answered is a paragraph.

This paragraph replaces or extends the Fit paragraph. It does not go in addition to it, because the letter is still 350 words.

### 5.1 An employment gap

```text
Weak:   Due to unforeseen personal circumstances I was unable to work between March
        2024 and August 2025, but I have remained passionate about the industry.

Strong: I was out of work from March 2024 to August 2025, caring for a family member
        full time. During it I kept my Kubernetes certification current, shipped two
        pull requests to the Ktor client that are in the 3.x releases, and took on a
        part-time contract rebuilding an inventory sync in the last four months. I am
        available full time now and have been since June.
```

Note what the strong version does not do: it does not explain, justify or over-share. One clause on the reason, then evidence, then a clear statement of current availability — which is the thing the reader actually wants to know and the thing a gap makes them uncertain about.

### 5.2 A career change

The reader's fear is that you are guessing about what the new job involves. Answer that fear specifically: name the transferable core, then show one instance of doing the new work, not of preparing to do it. A shipped project beats any number of courses, and a course with nothing shipped after it reads as a hobby.

### 5.3 A requirement you do not meet

Name the requirement, state the nearest true thing, and give the ramp honestly.

```text
Weak:   While I do not have direct experience with Rust, I am a fast learner and am
        confident I could pick it up quickly.

Strong: I have not shipped Rust in production. I have shipped eight years of C++ with
        the same ownership and lifetime problems solved by hand, and I rewrote our
        image pipeline's hot path in Rust as a side project last year — it is on
        GitHub and it is where I learned how much of my C++ instinct was wrong.
```

"Fast learner" is an unfalsifiable adjective and carries no weight. A thing you built carries all of it. Where a stated requirement is genuinely load-bearing — a licence, a language, a clearance, a work permit — a letter does not fix it, and it may well be a knockout question further up the form.

### 5.4 Overqualification and relocation

**Overqualification.** The unspoken concern is that you will leave in six months or resent the scope. Name the reason you want a smaller role, and make it a reason a stranger can believe: a deliberate move out of management, a domain you want to work in, a location constraint. Vagueness here confirms the fear.

**Relocation.** State that you are moving, when, and at whose cost. An employer's real question is whether they are being asked to fund a visa or a move, and an unanswered question is assumed to be expensive. If you already hold the right to work, say so in six words in the first paragraph, not in the last.

**The resume side of all of this** — how to write the dates, why a functional resume fails both the parser and the reader, what to leave for the conversation — is [patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md](../patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md). The two documents are deliberately split: the resume decides whether the gap is noticed, and the letter decides what it means.

## 6. Format and delivery

The letter is read by a person, so none of the parser constraints in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md) apply to it. The rules that remain are about looking like one coherent package.

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Format** | Plain text, or a simple document in the same font as the resume. No template, no letterhead graphics, no columns. |
| **Contact header** | Identical to the resume's, so the two documents read as one package rather than two arrivals. |
| **Addressee** | A named person where you can find one — the hiring manager, the team lead, the founder. A team where you cannot: "Dear Engineering Hiring Team". **Never "To whom it may concern"**, which announces that you did not look. |
| **Getting the name wrong** | Worse than not using one. If you are not confident, use the team. |
| **Length** | One page. 250 to 350 words. A two-page letter is not read at all. |
| **File name** | Same convention as the resume: `Firstname-Lastname-Cover-Letter.pdf`. |
| **Text box versus attachment** | Where the form offers a box, **paste plain text into the box.** An attachment is one more click, and the click is where the read is lost. Where both exist, do both. |
| **Pasting** | Check what survived the paste. Smart quotes, em-dashes and bullet glyphs occasionally arrive as junk in a plain-text field, and a letter opening with a mojibake character has already made its impression. |
| **Email body** | When applying by email, the letter *is* the email body. Do not attach a letter to an email that says "please find attached my letter", which is a letter about a letter. |

### 6.1 When the form replaces the letter

The modern application form has largely absorbed the cover letter into free-text custom questions: *why are you interested in this role*, *what is the piece of work you are proudest of*, *tell us about a time you disagreed with a technical decision*. Treat these as the letter, because that is what they are — and treat them as the better version of it, because unlike an attachment they are guaranteed to be looked at by whoever reviews the application.

- **Answer them with the same material.** One story, one number, one constraint, one thing specific to this company. The paragraph you would have written for section 4.2 usually drops straight in.
- **Respect the length signal.** A box with a small character limit is asking for three sentences, and filling it to the character is not the flex it feels like. A box with no limit is not an invitation to write a page.
- **They cannot auto-reject you.** Free text is outside what auto-reject rules can act on (section 1.1). What they can do is be read by a person, which is the point.
- **Do not answer a specific question generically.** "Tell us about a technical disagreement" answered with a paragraph about your passion for collaboration is a worse outcome than leaving it blank, because it is a direct demonstration of not answering questions.
- **Where both a box and an attachment field exist**, put the letter in the box and attach it too. The attachment is the archive copy; the box is the one that gets read.

Which systems present these questions, and how the answers are stored and reviewed, is in [references/ats-catalog.md](../references/ats-catalog.md).

## 7. The AI line

This is the part most people get wrong in both directions, so state it precisely.

**The emerging norm is that AI as an editor is accepted and AI as a ghostwriter is rejected.** Using a model to tighten, cut and fix your English is now unremarkable and is not what reviewers object to. Handing a model the job posting and getting back a letter is what they object to, and what they say they can spot.

### 7.1 The tell is not what people think it is

**It is not prose style.** A non-native English speaker producing clean, formal, slightly stiff English is not what gets flagged. That register has existed in applications for decades and reviewers read it as care. Advice to "write worse English so it looks human" is wrong, and it is actively harmful to anyone applying across a language border.

**The tell is the absence of specifics.** What marks a letter as machine-written is that nothing in it could only have been written by this candidate: no number, no named system, no constraint that was actually binding, no trade-off, and no reference to the company that could not be pasted into an application to a competitor. A model given a posting and no facts about you produces text that is fluent, correct and interchangeable — and interchangeability is the signal.

This is why section 4.3's swap test doubles as an AI test. A letter that passes the swap test is, by construction, a letter a model could not have written from the posting alone.

The phrase list — the specific openings, closings and connective tissue that now read as generated — is in [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md) under the generated-text group. It is maintained in one place and is not repeated here. Search your draft for each of those phrases before sending and delete every hit.

### 7.2 The workflow that stays on the right side of the line

1. **Write the specifics yourself, badly.** Bullet points are fine. The number, the constraint, the trade-off, the thing about this company. Nobody else has these.
2. **Let the model turn them into prose**, with an explicit instruction to add no facts.
3. **Delete everything it added anyway.** Models fill gaps with plausible generic enthusiasm, and that is exactly the material that gets you flagged.
4. **Run the swap test**, then the phrase search.
5. **Read it aloud.** If a sentence is one you would not say to the hiring manager's face, cut it.

Step 3 is the one people skip and it is the one that matters.

### 7.3 And the obvious warning

Never put hidden text, white-on-white instructions or prompt injections in a cover letter — the same rule as for a resume, for the same reasons, plus one specific to letters: a cover letter is the document a human is most likely to actually read, which makes it the worst possible place to hide something. The measured prevalence, the detection tooling and the game theory are covered in [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md).

## 8. Where the market changes the answer

Everything above assumes the anglophone convention, in which a letter is optional and frequently unread. That assumption does not hold everywhere, and the difference is large enough to change the decision in section 3.

- **DACH:** effectively obligatory. The `Anschreiben` is the piece the reader opens first, and its absence reads as an incomplete application rather than a stylistic choice.
- **France, Italy, the Netherlands:** conventional and expected, though the weight given to it varies by sector.
- **US and UK technology:** optional, frequently unread at the screening stage, and the market this document's default advice is calibrated to.

The country-by-country detail, along with photograph and personal-data conventions, is [references/market-conventions.md](../references/market-conventions.md). This document only tells you that the default flips.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I attached the same letter to forty applications to save time" | A generic letter measured barely above no letter at all in 2020, and today it signals a generated document. It spends the one read you were going to get on proof that you did not look. |
| "I skipped it because the field said optional" | Someone configured that field deliberately. An optional field is a filter, and an empty one is a visible choice rather than a neutral one. |
| "I put keywords in my cover letter so it would score well" | Nothing scores it. No parser extracts it and no auto-reject rule can act on it. You optimised a document no machine reads. |
| "I opened with 'I am writing to express my strong interest'" | The single most-flagged opening of 2026. It contains no information and it is what a model writes when it has no facts. |
| "My letter summarises my resume" | They already have the resume, in the same application, and it is better at being a resume. A paragraph that adds no facts wasted the read. |
| "I wrote that I am passionate about their mission and values" | It passes no test: swap the company name and the sentence survives intact, which is exactly the interchangeability that reads as machine-written. |
| "I didn't mention the gap — better not to draw attention to it" | The reader sees the dates on the resume regardless. Undiscussed, they form their own theory, and it is worse than yours. An objection you raise and answer is a paragraph; one they raise is a rejection. |
| "I said I was a fast learner for the skill I don't have" | Unfalsifiable and weightless. One shipped thing in the missing technology outperforms every adjective available. |
| "I addressed it to whom it may concern" | It announces that you did not spend two minutes looking. A team name costs nothing and does not carry that signal. |
| "I had AI write the whole thing — the English is perfect" | Perfect English is not the tell. The absence of a number, a constraint or anything specific to this company is, and a generated letter has none of those by construction. |
| "I wrote two pages because I had a lot to explain" | Two pages is not read. If the explanation needs more than a paragraph, it is an interview topic, not a letter topic. |
