# The Top Third: Headline, Summary and Value Proposition

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global, us, uk, eu** | **ATS: vendor-neutral**

**Read when:** what goes at the top · summary or headline · is the objective dead · my summary says nothing · what do they read first · stating remote or relocation · how long the summary should be

**Not this file:** [guides/02-resume-anatomy.md](02-resume-anatomy.md) — every other section, its order and its length. This document covers only the block above the first section heading, which is the part that decides whether the rest is read.

## TL;DR

- **The branded headline, the advertising-style headline, the branding statement and the value proposition are one object, not four techniques.** It is a line naming the role you are applying for plus the strongest verifiable evidence that fits beside it. Learn it once.
- **Default: a headline line plus two summary bullets for technical roles; a headline plus two or three sentences of prose where narrative positioning carries the case** — a career change, a management role, an executive search.
- **Kill the objective.** It states what you want at the exact moment the reader is deciding whether you are relevant, and every source in the field has called it obsolete for a decade.
- **You are designing for a thirty-second skim, not a six-second glance.** Covert timing of 114 recruiters measured 17 to 46 seconds depending on length. That is a materially different brief: you have room for substance and no room for a slogan.
- **Put the target job title on its own line under your name.** It is the single highest-leverage string in the document, and it must be the title being hired for rather than the one your HR system prints.
- **One line to re-aim the whole document.** Everything role-specific belongs in the headline, so tailoring is a one-line edit — which is the only version of tailoring that actually gets done.

## 1. Four names, one object

Read four authors on resumes and you meet this object four times under four names, described as if it were four separate techniques. It is not. It is one thing, and the confusion costs readers more than any other terminological muddle in the field, because it makes the most valuable area of the document feel like an unsettled question.

| Name in the source | Tradition it comes from | How it is defined there | What it uniquely contributes | What it gets wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Branded headline** | Professional resume writing | Target title, plus credentials, plus areas of expertise, plus a value tagline; a supporting statement capped at about twenty words | The word cap, and the idea that the line has named slots rather than being free prose | Four slots invite stacking until the line is no longer scannable — the exact failure the form exists to prevent |
| **Headline** | Advertising copywriting, via the classic principle that far more people read the headline than read the body | Target job title plus one quantified result — a seniority-and-domain title followed by a concrete scaled outcome | The demand for a number, and the re-targeting property: the whole document is re-aimed by editing one line | Treats the resume as an advertisement, which invites claims the body cannot evidence |
| **Branding statement** | Executive resume practice | A short third-person statement of capability, no first-person pronouns, no aspiration | The register — what you *are*, not what you want to become | Degenerates into corporate boilerplate the moment the capability is not specific |
| **Value proposition** | Sales and consulting, framing "the upper third" | Whatever answers the reader's implicit question, *can you solve my problem?* | The only one of the four that gives a pass/fail test rather than a template | Says nothing about form, so on its own it cannot be executed |

### 1.1 The definition, stated once

Call it the **positioning line**. It is the first line of your document after your name and title, and it is:

```text
[Target job title] — [the strongest verifiable evidence that fits on the same line]
```

Five rules, taken from the four traditions above and from nothing else:

1. **One line.** Under about twenty words. If it wraps to three, it is a summary, not a headline, and it will be skimmed as prose.
2. **The title is the target's title**, not your internal one. See section 5.1.
3. **The evidence half contains at least one thing that could be false** — a number, a named system, a scale, a named employer. A clause nobody could dispute is decoration.
4. **No first-person pronouns and no aspiration.** This is the branding-statement contribution and it is worth keeping: "Seeking a role where I can" fails before the reader reaches the verb.
5. **It passes the reader's question or it does not exist.** Read it as a hiring manager with a stack of applications: does it move you from *who is this* to *plausibly right for this, keep reading*? If not, no amount of formula compliance saves it.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** the specific numbers in these formulas — twenty words for the supporting statement, three areas of expertise, a headline of exactly one line — are presented in the source literature as if validated. None of the four traditions cites an experiment, and no field study isolates the top block from the document under it. Follow the caps anyway: they are cheap, they enforce compression, and compression is the one property the attention evidence in section 3 actually supports. But know that you are following a convention, not a finding.

### 1.2 The property worth having: one line to re-target

The advertising-derived version contributes something operational the other three do not. If everything role-specific lives in one line, aiming the document at a different job is a one-line edit plus a reordering of the skills block.

That matters because of what tailoring costs in practice. A four-sentence prose summary written for a platform-engineering role has to be rewritten, sentence by sentence, for an SRE role — and it does not get rewritten. It gets sent as it is, describing a slightly different candidate than the one the reader is looking for. A structure whose tailoring cost is one line is a structure that stays tailored. The full workflow is in [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](05-keywords-and-tailoring.md); the point here is that the headline form is what makes that workflow survivable.

## 2. What physically goes at the top

Underneath the naming confusion sits a real disagreement, and it is worth stating plainly because the four positions are genuinely incompatible.

| Form | What it looks like | Choose it when | How it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prose Professional Summary** | Three or four sentences under a `Summary` heading | The case needs a narrative — a career change, a manager or executive role, an unusual history that needs framing before the dates are read | Reads as a paragraph, and a paragraph is the first thing a skimmer skips. Degrades into adjectives faster than any other form |
| **Headline replacing the summary** | One line under the name and title; no summary section at all | Strong, obvious, linear track record; the reader needs orientation, not persuasion | Carries no context. At senior level the reader wants to know the scope you operate at, and one line cannot say it |
| **Two summary bullets** | Two bullets under `Summary`: one condensing years and core capability, one carrying the single most impressive result | Technical individual-contributor roles at any level | Looks unconventional to a traditional HR reviewer in conservative sectors, and gives you exactly two slots — the second one has to be genuinely your best |
| **No summary at all** | Contact block, then straight into Skills or Experience | One-page junior resume where a summary would restate the only two jobs on the page | The reader has to infer what you are from the job titles. Where your current title does not match the target title, that inference goes wrong |

### 2.1 The resolution

**For technical roles, use a headline line plus two summary bullets. That is the default.**

```text
Marta Rossi
Senior Backend Engineer — Kotlin and JVM services at 40k requests/second

Summary
- Backend engineer, 9 years, payments and high-throughput APIs; Kotlin, Java, Postgres, AWS.
- Cut p99 checkout latency from 900 ms to 220 ms and led the migration of the
  payments platform serving 3M monthly users, with no customer-visible downtime.
```

The two bullets do specific jobs and are not interchangeable. The first is the **identity bullet**: years, domain, the stack. It answers *what are you*. The second is the **proof bullet**: the single strongest outcome you own, with its number attached. It answers *are you any good*. Everything else you were going to write in a prose summary is either repeated below or is an adjective.

Two of the practitioner sources arrive at this form independently for technical roles, and one describes it as a deliberate break with tradition that senior technical recruiters prefer. Treat that as practitioner report rather than measurement — nobody has run the comparison. The mechanistic argument is stronger than the sourcing: bullets are what the rest of the document is made of, a skimmer's eye is already calibrated to them, and a bullet cannot hide a missing claim inside a subordinate clause the way a sentence can.

**Use prose where positioning is the work.** A career changer, an engineering manager, a candidate returning after a break, and any executive search: in each case the reader's first question is not *what are you* but *why does this history add up to the job I am filling*, and that question needs sentences. Keep it to three, keep it third-person, and put the reframing in the first one rather than the last. Gap and career-change framing itself belongs in [patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md](../patterns/career-gaps-and-changes.md).

**Use the headline alone** when the document is one page, the track record is linear and the titles speak for themselves. **Use no top block at all** almost never — the one honest case is a junior one-pager where the summary would only restate the two jobs printed underneath it.

### 2.2 Choosing, in one pass

| Your situation | Form | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Technical individual contributor, any level | Headline plus two summary bullets | The reader is sizing your scope and your best result, and both are countable |
| Engineering or product manager | Headline plus three prose sentences | Scope, team shape and one delivery outcome need connective tissue that bullets strip out |
| Career change, or a return after a break | Headline plus three prose sentences | The reframing is the argument, and an argument needs sentences |
| Executive or director search | Headline plus prose, third person throughout | The register is a convention of that market and violating it reads as junior |
| First job, one page, two entries | Headline only, no summary section | A summary would restate the page |
| Contract or agency history | Headline plus two bullets, identity bullet naming the engagement shape | The reader's first question is what kind of worker you are, before what you did |

Where two of these describe you, prefer prose: the cost of prose is that it gets skimmed, and the cost of bullets is that they cannot carry a reframing.

### 2.3 The principle underneath, which is the part to remember

The top block exists to answer two questions in a few seconds: **what are you, and are you plausibly right for this?**

Any form that answers them works. Any form that opens with self-description does not, whatever its shape. "Results-driven professional with a passion for excellence" fails identically as a paragraph, as a headline, as a bullet and as a branding statement, because the failure is not formal. It is that the reader finishes the line knowing nothing they did not know before reading it.

That is why the four-way argument about form is smaller than it looks, and why this document spends more space on section 6 than on the choice between prose and bullets.

## 3. Where attention actually lands

This is the neighbourhood the field's second-most-repeated myth lives in, so it needs handling carefully.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** "recruiters spend six seconds on a resume". The origin is known — a 2012 vendor eye-tracking exercise with approximately thirty participants who were instructed toward speed, never peer-reviewed and never replicated — but the claim as it circulates is not supported by what was done. The claim-by-claim audit is in [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md); it is named here only because it is the reason people write billboards instead of resumes.

The best available measurement says something different and more useful:

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, covert timing via session recording, 2023):** each participant was shown a job description, then three resumes of different lengths presented as images to prevent text search, without knowing they were being timed. Attention measured 17 seconds for a short one-page resume, 27 seconds for a mid-length one and 46 seconds for a long one. Fastest observed pass: 12 seconds. Slowest: two minutes and twenty-seven seconds.

**The shortest observed average is double the mythical figure, and the longest is nearly eight times it.** The covert timing is what makes this the stronger instrument, not the larger sample: removing the participant's awareness removes the demand characteristic that shaped the 2012 result.

### 3.1 What that changes about the brief

Designing for six seconds and designing for thirty are different jobs.

| Six-second brief | Thirty-second brief |
|---|---|
| One idea, maximum | An identity, one proof, and a route into the body |
| Slogan register | Evidence register |
| Everything else is noise to be removed | The rest of page one is read, so it must reward being read |
| Numbers are decoration you cannot afford | Numbers are the substance, because they survive a skim intact |

And the skim is a skim for a structural reason, not because recruiters are careless:

> **Study (6,000+ companies, 640M+ applications, 2022 to 2025):** applications per job rose from 116 to 244 while applications handled per recruiter per year rose from 146 to 746, as the number of recruiters per organisation fell from 10.43 to 4.62.

The volume reality and what it means for the whole funnel is the subject of [guides/01-how-screening-actually-works.md](01-how-screening-actually-works.md). Its consequence for this document is narrow and worth stating: the reader of your top block is triaging, and a triaging reader looks for reasons to stop. Every unfalsifiable line is one.

A thirty-second reader has time for your title, your headline, two bullets, and the employer-and-title column of your last two jobs. Write for that reader. The six-second framing produces documents that are *thin* — a slogan followed by white space — and thin documents lose to dense ones at exactly the moment the reader turns out to have had half a minute.

### 3.2 Where on the page

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, session heat-mapping, 2023):** in the same work, attention concentrated on the first page and particularly on its upper half. Later pages were skim-read rather than skipped — longer resumes received *more* total attention, not less, which is the opposite of what the six-second framing implies.

Two scanning patterns are described in this literature: an **F-shape**, the more common, where the eye reads across the top, drops, reads a shorter span, then runs down the left margin; and a **Z-shape**, which appears with visually structured layouts where distinct blocks pull the eye diagonally. Be honest about the provenance: the F- and Z-patterns come from eye-tracking work on web reading, applied to resumes by practitioners. No resume-specific study with a disclosed method has confirmed them. They are a plausible model, not a finding.

What survives from the weak 2012 exercise is its *spatial* claim rather than its temporal one, because gaze location is what the instrument was actually built to measure: roughly 80% of initial attention landed on name, current title and company, previous title and company, employment dates and education. Every element in that list is either in the top block or in the first two lines of the first job entry.

And one fabrication in the same neighbourhood, which matters because it is about this exact document: a widely cited "2023 University of Michigan eye-tracking study" reporting six seconds with 70% of attention on the top third does not appear to exist. It is a real institution's name attached to a rounder version of the 2012 numbers. Do not repeat it, and be suspicious of any advice whose case for the top third rests on it.

## 4. The layout the evidence supports

The sources agree on the layout consequence even where they disagree about everything else:

1. **Identity and contact top-left.** Name, target title, then the contact line.
2. **A distinguishing signal top-right** — the one thing that separates you from the stack.
3. **The strongest content on the diagonal** running from that top-left identity block down through the headline and the first bullets.
4. **History and education below**, in that order for anyone past their first job.

### 4.1 The conflict with single-column, and how to resolve it

Point two is where this advice collides with the parser. "Top-right" is usually implemented as a second column or a floated block, and a multi-column layout is a fatal-band finding in the only published parser quality specification — with content from one cell spliced into another rather than merely orphaned. The mechanics are in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md) and they are not negotiable.

**The resolution: the diagonal is a description of how eyes move over a page, not an instruction to build a two-column header.** Put the distinguishing signal on the title line directly under your name. That position is on the diagonal, it is in the linear text flow, and it is the first line the parser reads after the name.

```text
Marta Rossi
Senior Backend Engineer — payments, 40k requests/second
marta.rossi@example.com | +39 340 000 0000 | Milan, Italy | linkedin.com/in/martarossi
Open to remote (CET ±3) and to relocation within the EU
```

If you want the visual top-right effect for the human-facing version of your document, that is what the two-document strategy is for — see [patterns/two-document-strategy.md](../patterns/two-document-strategy.md). Never buy it on the copy that goes into a portal.

### 4.2 The contact line, briefly

The contact block is the first two lines of the top third and it is mostly a mechanical problem rather than a writing one, so the rules live in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md) and are not restated here. Four points belong in this document because they are editorial decisions rather than parser constraints:

1. **One line, not a stack.** Email, phone with its international prefix, city and country, and the one or two profile URLs that are actually relevant. Five lines of contact detail pushes your proof below the fold to buy nothing.
2. **Include only links a reader will open.** For an engineer that is usually a repository host and nothing else. A personal site that redirects to a landing page, a Twitter handle you last used four years ago, and a Medium profile with two posts each cost a fifth of a line and return nothing.
3. **City and country, never the street.** Street-level detail is a suggested-improvement finding in the published quality codes, and it answers a question nobody asked at this stage.
4. **Your name is text, at the largest size on the page, and nothing else is.** A name rendered inside a logo or a graphic is a major-band finding and it is the one field the entire record hangs from.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Textkernel's parser emits a major-band finding when contact information is located anywhere other than the top of the document, and a fatal one when neither an email address nor a phone number is found at all.

## 5. What the top block contains

### 5.1 The title line

One line, directly under your name, carrying the job title you are applying for.

> **Study (n=384 HR professionals directly involved in hiring, random sampling, 2025):** job titles are the third most-used filter inside the ATS at 55.3%, behind skills at 76.4% and educational background at 59.7%.

This is the highest-leverage string in the document, and the most common way people waste it is by printing the title their HR system gave them. `Member of Technical Staff III`, `Software Engineer P4` and `Digital Solutions Consultant` exist in one company's vocabulary and are searched by nobody.

```text
Before: Member of Technical Staff III
After:  Senior Software Engineer (internally Member of Technical Staff III)
```

Write the market-standard title, and where the internal one is on your payslip and might come up in a reference check, put it in parentheses. The rule and its limit — translation is legitimate, promotion is not — are set out in [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md).

Three refinements:

- **Match the posting's title where it is honest to do so.** If the posting says `Backend Engineer` and you would naturally write `Software Engineer, Backend`, use theirs. This is not gaming; it is answering in the language of the question.
- **Do not stack.** `Senior Backend Engineer | Team Lead | Cloud Architect | Mentor` reads as someone who is none of these things. One title. Specialisms go in the headline's evidence half.
- **Never seniority-inflate the title line.** It is the first claim on the document and the cheapest one in the whole process to check.

### 5.2 The headline and the two to four lines under it

Two to four lines total, carrying three things: **years, domain, and the two or three outcomes that distinguish you.** Nothing else earns the space.

| Slot | Budget | What good looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Years and domain | Half a line | `Backend engineer, 9 years, payments and high-throughput APIs` |
| Stack or scope | Half a line | The four or five terms that define the role, not the whole skills block |
| The proof | One to two lines | One outcome with a number, or one system with a scale |
| Availability | One line, optional | See 5.4 |

Keep the whole block short for a mechanical reason as well as a rhetorical one: a non-standard section larger than the work and education sections combined is a major-band parser finding. A summary that runs to eight lines is competing with your career for the reader's page-one budget and losing.

### 5.3 Keywords, and an honest statement of how much they help here

Put the terms that define the role in this block — the title, the domain, the three or four technologies the job is actually about — and write them as English rather than as a list.

But be clear about what that does and does not buy, because the source literature oversells it:

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Textkernel's candidate data model attributes each extracted skill to the job or education entry it appeared in, and derives from that attribution the years of experience for that skill and the date it was last used. A skill named in a floating block acquires no duration and no provenance.

A skill mentioned only in your summary is therefore the *weakest* placement available: it has no dated entry to attach to. No vendor documents a positional weight for the summary block. What the top block genuinely earns you is human attention and whole-document relevance to a ranking layer that reads the file as text — which is worth having, and is not the same as populating the filter facet a recruiter searches on. The three-placement rule that resolves this properly is in [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](05-keywords-and-tailoring.md).

**The practical consequence:** two or three role-defining terms up top, the rest in the skills block, and every one of them also inside a dated bullet.

### 5.4 The availability line

Where it is relevant, state location and remote or relocation status as a literal line in the contact block. Several sources recommend it and they are right, for a mechanical reason.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Workday Recruiting exposes twelve mapped eligibility questions available for disqualification, among them willingness to relocate and availability. Answers generate calculated fields usable in Automatic Stage Routing condition rules and can carry positive or negative scores.

The line answers a knockout question before it is asked, and it removes the ambiguity that otherwise gets resolved against you by a recruiter clearing a queue. Location is also a filter in its own right, used by 43.4% of the same HR sample.

```text
Milan, Italy · Open to remote (CET ±3) and to relocation within the EU
Berlin, Germany · Hybrid or on-site, no relocation
```

Work authorisation is a separate and sharper case, with its own phrasing rules and its own trap for cross-border applications — that treatment is in [references/market-conventions.md](../references/market-conventions.md) and is not repeated here. Put the authorisation string on the same line as the availability one when both apply.

One restraint: the availability line is for facts that answer a filter. `Open to remote` is a fact. `Open to exciting opportunities in a range of sectors` is an objective statement wearing a disguise, and it belongs in section 6.1.

### 5.5 What to call the section, and where the heading goes

Two decisions, and the sources conflate them.

- **The headline takes no heading.** It is part of the identity block, sitting directly under your name and title, and a heading above a single line is scaffolding the reader has to step over.
- **The summary, in either form, takes a heading, and the heading is `Summary`.** Not because it reads better — it barely matters to a human — but because a block of content with no heading above it is a major-band parser finding in its own right, and a parser that cannot see where the summary ends may attribute its text to whatever section follows.

`Summary` and `Professional Summary` are both recognised. `Profile` and `About Me` are riskier strings, `Career Objective` reintroduces the word you just deleted, and anything inventive — `Elevator Pitch`, `Who I Am`, `My Story` — risks the section not being identified at all. The full heading table, with the parser codes behind it, is in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md).

### 5.6 What the block must claim at each rung

The top block is where mispositioning by seniority happens, because it is the only place in the document that makes a direct claim about what level you operate at. The full treatment of level expectations is in [patterns/seniority-ladder.md](../patterns/seniority-ladder.md); what follows is only what changes in this block.

| Rung | What the top block must establish | The usual mispositioning |
|---|---|---|
| Junior | What you can build, in what, with evidence that real users touched it | `Aspiring`, a degree-first opening, and coursework where an outcome should be |
| Mid | Domain, stack, and one outcome you personally owned end to end | A list of responsibilities, which reads as a job description rather than a record |
| Senior | The scope and blast radius of the decisions you make, and the systems you own | Still writing a mid-level feature list, one level of ambition below the title |
| Staff or principal | The class of problem you get called for, and influence beyond your own team | A headline claiming architecture with proof that only demonstrates delivery |
| Engineering manager | Teams, headcount, domain, and one delivery metric beside one people metric | A technical headline on a people job, which reads as a reluctant manager |

The pattern across the rungs is consistent: **the more senior the role, the more the top block has to be about scope and the less it can be about tools.** A principal engineer whose headline is a list of four languages has described a mid-level candidate.

## 6. What must never go there

### 6.1 The objective

Every source in the field calls it obsolete, and for once they are unanimous and correct.

```text
Before: Objective: To obtain a challenging position at a forward-thinking
company where I can leverage my skills and grow professionally.

After:  Data Engineer — Python, Spark, dbt. Moved nightly reporting from
6 hours to 40 minutes for a 300-person analytics organisation.
```

The mechanism of the failure is worth naming rather than just asserting. The objective spends the most-read position in the document on **your** requirements at the exact moment the reader is deciding whether you are relevant to **theirs**. It is also the one part of a resume that is reliably identical across candidates, which makes it the fastest line on the page to stop reading. The single legitimate residue — that a reader sometimes cannot tell what role you are applying for — is fully handled by the title line in 5.1.

### 6.2 Unfalsifiable self-description

This is the substantive failure, and it survives every reform of the *form*.

```text
Before: Experienced engineer, passionate about building great products, with a
proven track record of delivering high-quality software in fast-paced environments.

After:  Senior Backend Engineer — Kotlin and JVM services at 40k requests/second.
Cut p99 checkout latency from 900 ms to 220 ms; led the payments migration for 3M
monthly users with no customer-visible downtime.
```

Count what the reader learns from the first version: nothing. Not the language, not the domain, not the scale, not one thing that happened. It would fit a graduate and a principal engineer equally well, which means it distinguishes neither. It also costs about six seconds of a thirty-second budget, so it is not merely useless — it is expensive.

The rewrite names the stack, the scale and a measured outcome, and every clause in it can be interrogated in an interview. That is the test: **if no interviewer could challenge a sentence, it is not carrying information.** The same principle drives bullet construction throughout the document — see [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](04-bullets-and-evidence.md).

The specific vocabulary to strike — `results-driven`, `proven track record`, `passionate`, `thrives in fast-paced environments`, `team player`, `detail-oriented` — is catalogued with its replacements in [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md). And note the second-order effect: this register is now also the strongest surface signal of a machine-written document, so it costs you twice.

### 6.3 The words that get you excluded from searches

`Seeking`, `Aspiring`, `Looking for`, `Unemployed`. Recruiters exclude these strings with a Boolean `NOT` clause, which removes your record from the result set rather than ranking it lower — you are absent, not rejected, and nothing tells you it happened.

The reason this belongs in *this* document, when the list itself lives in [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md), is that the headline is precisely where people write them. "Seeking a challenging role in software development" is the single most common opening line in the corpus of bad resumes, and it manages to be excluded from searches, uninformative, and an objective statement in disguise, all in seven words.

### 6.4 The rest of the delete list

| Do not put it in the top block | Why |
|---|---|
| A first-person paragraph — "I am a developer who…" | Wrong register for the document; the whole resume is written in implied first person without pronouns |
| Your photo, date of birth, marital status, full street address | Convention varies by market and none of it belongs at the top of an anglophone resume — see [references/market-conventions.md](../references/market-conventions.md) |
| A soft-skills list | Unfalsifiable by construction. Soft skills are demonstrated by outcomes below, never asserted above |
| A repeat of your top bullet, word for word | Wastes the block. If the achievement belongs in both places, compress it differently in each |
| Six certifications as an acronym string | They have their own section. In the top block they read as padding and push the proof line off the fold |
| "References available on request" | A suggested-improvement finding in the published parser quality codes, and it conveys nothing |
| Salary expectations | Never on the document. The form asks when it wants to know, and that is where the answer is evaluated |

## 7. Writing it

### 7.1 The procedure

1. **Write it last.** The top block is a compression of the document, and you cannot compress what you have not written. Drafting it first produces aspiration, because there is nothing yet to summarise.
2. **Open the posting and take the title verbatim** where it is honest. That is line one.
3. **Pull the two strongest bullets** from your history — strongest meaning largest verifiable number or largest scope, not most recent.
4. **Compress the best of them into the headline's evidence half.** One clause. If it needs two, it is the proof bullet, not the headline.
5. **Write the identity bullet:** years, domain, stack. Aim for one line.
6. **Cut every adjective** that does not survive the question *how would anyone check this*.
7. **Read the block cold, as the hiring manager.** If you finish it not knowing what the person is, start again from step three with a different bullet.
8. **Re-target per application by editing line one and the headline's evidence clause.** Nothing else in the block should need to change; if it does, your headline is carrying content that belongs in the body.

### 7.2 Three more before-and-after pairs

A career changer, where prose earns its place:

```text
Before: Motivated career changer seeking to transition into data analysis.
Recently completed a bootcamp and eager to apply my new skills.

After:  Data Analyst — SQL, Python, dbt · six years in clinical operations.
Built the reporting that replaced a manual weekly process for 40 clinics,
cutting the reporting cycle from three days to under two hours. Moving into
analytics full time after doing it inside operations for two years.
```

The rewrite works because it stops announcing the transition and starts evidencing it: the analytics work already happened, inside the previous job, at a stated scale.

An engineering manager, where scope is the claim:

```text
Before: Engineering leader with a passion for building high-performing teams
and delivering business value through technology.

After:  Engineering Manager — three teams, 21 engineers, platform and payments.
Took deploy frequency from fortnightly to daily and cut regretted attrition to
one engineer in two years while the group doubled.
```

Scope first — teams, headcount, domain — because that is what a manager is measured on and what the reader is trying to size. The proof line then carries one delivery metric and one people metric, which is the pair that a management reader is looking for and will not find in "high-performing teams".

A first job, where the temptation is to describe the ambition instead of the work:

```text
Before: Aspiring software developer, recent computer science graduate, eager to
learn and passionate about technology. Seeking my first role in the industry.

After:  Junior Backend Developer — Python, FastAPI, Postgres. Built and shipped
the booking service for a university society of 800 members, still in use;
six months of production support during a summer internship at a logistics firm.
```

Two words in the first version — `Aspiring` and `Seeking` — are enough on their own to remove the document from a recruiter's result set, and the rest of it describes a state of mind rather than a thing that exists. The rewrite claims less and evidences all of it: something was built, someone uses it, and there was a stretch of real production exposure. That is a stronger junior position than any adjective available.

### 7.3 Testing it before you send it

Three tests, in ascending order of how uncomfortable they are.

1. **The cover test.** Print page one. Cover everything below the top third with a sheet of paper. Read what is left for five seconds and ask what role this person is applying for and what the best thing they have done is. If either answer is absent, the block has failed at its only job.
2. **The extraction test.** Run the file through a text extractor and read the first twelve lines of output. That is what a parser and a whole-document ranking layer see, in that order, with your layout removed. If your name is not line one, or the title has landed after the contact details, or the headline has been spliced into an address, fix the layout rather than the words. The commands are in [references/verification-toolkit.md](../references/verification-toolkit.md).
3. **The stranger test.** Give the covered page to someone who hires for the role you want and does not know your history. Ask them what they would guess your level is. A candidate whose block reads one level below their actual seniority is the most common outcome, and it is invisible from the inside because you know what you meant.

A fourth, for the honest: read your block next to the posting. If you could swap in any of the other applicants' names and the block would still be true, you have written a category description rather than a position.

### 7.4 The self-check

Five questions. A no to any of them means the block is not finished.

- Does line one name the job being hired for?
- Can a reader say what you are after five seconds, without reading the body?
- Is there at least one number, scale or named system above the fold?
- Could an interviewer challenge every clause? If any clause is unchallengeable, delete it.
- Would this block be false, or at least wrong, for the candidate who sat next to you? If it would fit them too, it is not positioning — it is filler.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I read about branded headlines, value propositions and branding statements and I'm not sure which to use" | They are one object described by four traditions. Write the positioning line in section 1.1 and ignore the vocabulary |
| "I have six seconds, so I made the top a one-line slogan" | Covert timing of 114 recruiters measured 17 to 46 seconds. A slogan followed by white space loses to a dense block at the moment the reader turns out to have had half a minute |
| "My summary says I'm a results-driven professional with a proven track record" | The reader finishes it knowing nothing. It fits a graduate and a principal equally, costs a fifth of the attention budget, and now also reads as machine-written |
| "I put my objective at the top so they know what I want" | It spends the most-read line on your requirements while the reader is assessing their own. The title line does the useful half of the job |
| "My headline says Seeking new opportunities" | Recruiters exclude `seeking` with a Boolean `NOT`. You are removed from the result set, not ranked lower, and nothing tells you |
| "I used my real job title, Member of Technical Staff III" | It exists in one company's vocabulary. Job titles are the third most-used ATS filter at 55.3%, and nobody searches for that string |
| "I put my key achievements in a nice box at the top right" | A boxed or columnar header is a fatal-band parser finding, and cell content splices into neighbouring cells rather than merely being orphaned |
| "I listed twenty skills in the summary so the keywords are up front" | Skills in a floating block acquire no years and no recency. The filter reads the skills field; the summary is for the human |
| "I wrote the summary first, then the rest of the resume" | You compressed a document that did not exist yet, which is why it came out as aspiration |
| "I dropped the summary entirely — the jobs speak for themselves" | Only if your current title matches the target title. Where it does not, you have handed the reader an inference and they will get it wrong |
