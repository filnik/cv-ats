# What Changes by Seniority

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global** | **ATS: vendor-neutral**

**Read when:** twelve years of experience and mid-level offers · your resume reads junior · writing a staff resume · moving into management · a graduate with nothing to put on it · you were downlevelled before interviewing · three promotions, one employer

**Not this file:** [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](../guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md) — the formula for writing any single bullet. This one decides what your bullets have to be *about*, which is the part that changes with the rung.

## TL;DR

- **A junior resume and a staff resume are different documents, not the same document at different lengths.** The unit of accomplishment changes at every rung, and so does the subject of the sentence.
- **The most expensive failure in this field is a well-executed mid-level resume submitted for a senior role.** It reads as a mid-level candidate regardless of how many years are on it, because everything on it is implementation.
- **At senior, write the decision — the constraint, the alternative you rejected, the consequence.** A bullet that only says what was built could have been written by whoever was handed the ticket.
- **At staff and principal, concentrate rather than accumulate.** Depth in fewer technologies is more compelling than superficial knowledge of many, and education shrinks to one line.
- **A manager's metrics are different in kind, not in size:** team size, hires, promotions of reports, retention, delivery cadence, cost. A manager writing like an engineer reads as someone who has not let go.
- **Split every promotion into its own dated entry.** Progression is the one claim on your document that other people already validated, and a merged block hides it completely.

## 1. The thesis, and the evidence for it

The usual mental model of seniority on a resume is a volume knob: more years, more bullets, more pages, bigger numbers. That model is why strong senior engineers get mid-level offers. What actually changes between rungs is not the amount of evidence but **what counts as evidence** — and a document optimised for the wrong kind is not a weak document, it is a document for a different job.

The one piece of quantitative support for the shape of the argument is indirect, and it is worth reading for its gradient rather than its headline:

> **Study (n=482 recruiters, hiring managers, HR professionals and C-suite; 7,712 paired resume selections; 2018):** in a paired hiring simulation, reviewers were 2.3 times more likely to select the two-page version of an equivalent candidate. The effect was not flat across levels: 1.4x at entry level, 2.6x at mid-level and 2.9x for managerial roles.

The multiplier is soft — vendor-run, a simulation rather than a field experiment, and a two-page document carries more content by construction, so some of what it measures is content volume rather than page count. The **gradient** is the durable finding, and it is the one that matters here: what a reviewer wants from the document grows faster than the candidate's years do, and it grows in kind, not only in quantity. The length decision itself belongs to [guides/02-resume-anatomy.md](../guides/02-resume-anatomy.md); this document is about what fills the space.

**State the limitation plainly, because this KB's value is admitting it:** no controlled experiment tests seniority positioning. Everything below is drawn from three sources that are real but indirect — the published levelling rubrics that large employers use to calibrate loops, the structured fields recruiters actually filter on, and the mechanics of how a record is read. Treat it as a well-argued default, not as a measured effect.

### 1.1 What actually changes: the subject of the sentence

```text
Junior    I built …                     proof that you can build
Mid       I owned … end to end          proof that a thing given to you will land
Senior    I decided … because … which
          cost … and bought …           proof of judgement, and of raising others
Staff     I changed how N teams work,
          starting from an undefined
          problem                       proof of influence without authority
Manager   The team shipped …, and here
          is what happened to the
          people in it                  proof that you build the thing that builds
```

Everything else about resume writing is constant across the ladder: bullets are still evidence ([guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](../guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md)), the parser still needs a single column and clean dates ([references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md)), and the recruiter still retrieves you by structured field ([guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](../guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md)). Only the content of the claim moves.

### 1.2 The ladder in one table

| Rung | What the reviewer is testing | Unit of work | The document leads with | Characteristic mispositioning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Junior / graduate | Potential, and whether you build unprompted | A task, a feature under supervision | Education, then projects and internships written as work | A skills list of technologies met once |
| Mid | Independent delivery and depth in one stack | A feature or service, end to end | Experience; education drops to a line | A task list — what you were assigned, not what you decided |
| Senior | Autonomy, judgement at scale, raising the team | A system and the decisions inside it | The largest scope you have owned, with the decision in it | Implementation voice — reads as a strong mid-level candidate |
| Staff / principal | Influence beyond your team, work in ambiguity | An organisational problem, often undefined | Cross-team outcomes and the problems you framed | A longer senior resume, or a technology inventory |
| Engineering manager | Technical credibility plus people outcomes | A team, and its output over time | Team scope, then delivery and people outcomes | Writing like an engineer, or losing all technical content |

## 2. Junior and graduate

### 2.1 What the reviewer is testing

Potential, not track record. Two questions sit behind the review: *will this person be net-positive inside six months*, and *is there evidence they build things when nobody has assigned them anything*. Nothing on a graduate resume can answer the first directly, so the second carries the weight.

This is the only rung where the absence of professional experience is expected rather than explained. Do not apologise for it, do not pad around it, and do not use an objective statement to say you are eager to learn — see [guides/03-the-top-third.md](../guides/03-the-top-third.md) for why that block is expensive and what to put there instead.

### 2.2 What counts as evidence

| Evidence | How to write it | When to cut it |
|---|---|---|
| Degree | Institution, degree, graduation **year** only — never a date range | Never at this rung; it is your strongest dated fact |
| Relevant coursework | Only where a named course maps to a named requirement in the posting: a compilers course for a compilers job | Always, once you have a year of work, and immediately if it is generic |
| Thesis or capstone | One line describing the problem and the result, like a job bullet | Once it is not among your three strongest items |
| Internships | As real work: what shipped, who used it, what it replaced | Never — this is the closest thing to a track record you have |
| Projects | Something that solved a problem for someone, even one person | The moment it is a tutorial output (see 2.3) |
| Public code | Only where the repository is maintained: real commit history, a README, issues closed | If the profile is three abandoned repos, omit the link entirely |
| Competitions, open-source contributions, teaching | Merged PRs in a project you did not start are the highest-signal item on this list | — |

An empty GitHub link is worse than no link, because the reviewer clicks it. The same applies to a portfolio site that is a template with placeholder text still in it.

### 2.3 The tutorial-project rule

**A tutorial project is worse than no project at all.** This is the single most useful thing to tell a graduate and almost nobody says it.

A project built by following a course teaches the reviewer one thing: that you have not yet worked on anything of your own. It also fails in the interview, mechanically. The interviewer's opening move on any project is "why did you do it that way", and there is no answer available, because the decision was made by the instructor. A project with no decisions in it cannot demonstrate judgement, and demonstrating a small amount of judgement is the entire purpose of the section.

The tells a reviewer recognises instantly: the to-do app, the clone of a well-known product that thousands of course graduates also built, a repository whose entire commit history is one commit named `initial commit` dated the week you started applying.

The replacement is smaller than people expect. Something with one real user — a scraper your flatmate uses, a bot for a club you belong to, a script that automates a job you were doing by hand — beats a technically larger project with no user, because it comes with constraints, a decision, and something that broke.

```text
Before: E-commerce platform — built a full-stack shop with React, Node and Stripe
        following the Udemy course, with cart, checkout and admin panel.
After:  Booking tool for a 40-member climbing club, in use since March 2025.
        Replaced a shared spreadsheet that double-booked sessions; wrote the slot
        locking after the first weekend produced two conflicting bookings.
```

### 2.4 What the document leads with

Education sits above experience at this rung and only at this rung, and it moves below experience the moment you have a year of real work. Then projects, then internships or part-time work, then skills. Full ordering, including where the transition happens, is in [guides/02-resume-anatomy.md](../guides/02-resume-anatomy.md).

Write the internship as work, not as attendance. The most common graduate bullet describes presence in a building.

```text
Before: Summer intern on the payments team. Attended sprint planning and daily
        stand-ups, shadowed senior engineers and completed assigned tickets.
After:  Shipped the retry path for failed card authorisations to production during
        a ten-week internship; it removed a manual reconciliation the support team
        had been running twice a day.
```

### 2.5 The mispositioning: the encountered-once skills list

The characteristic graduate failure is a skills block listing every technology the candidate has ever opened — twenty items after eighteen months of coursework — and it collapses at the first technical question, because the interviewer picks from your list and does not pick the one you are best at.

It is also weaker than it looks to the machine, for a documented reason:

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Textkernel's candidate data model attributes each extracted skill to the specific job or education entry it appeared in, and derives from that attribution the years of experience for that skill and the date it was last used. A skill named in a floating block acquires no duration and no provenance.

A twenty-item list of bare strings is therefore twenty assertions with no evidence attached, and it dilutes the four items you could defend. Cut it to what you can be interviewed on, and mark the rest honestly.

```text
Before: Java, Python, Go, Rust, C++, JavaScript, TypeScript, React, Vue, Angular,
        Node, Django, Spring, Kubernetes, Docker, AWS, GCP, Terraform, Kafka, Redis
After:  Python (primary, 3 years), PostgreSQL, Django, Docker, Git
        Working knowledge: Go, AWS (Lambda, S3), React
```

The mechanics of writing the block so that it also populates the recruiter's filter facet are in [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](../guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md) and are not repeated here.

## 3. Mid-level

### 3.1 What the reviewer is testing

Whether a piece of work can be handed to you and land without supervision. That is the whole of the mid-level bar, and it has three components: depth in a stack rather than acquaintance with several, ownership of something end to end, and the first evidence that other people follow your technical opinions.

Experience displaces education here permanently. The degree becomes institution, degree, year, at the bottom of the page.

### 3.2 What counts as evidence

- **End-to-end ownership of a named thing.** Not "worked on the billing service" — owned it, including the parts nobody enjoys: the migration, the on-call rota, the deprecation of the thing it replaced.
- **Depth in one stack, visible through specificity.** Naming the failure mode you fixed proves depth in a way that naming the technology never does.
- **Independent delivery.** A project where the design, the sequencing and the rollout were yours, even if the goal was someone else's.
- **The first technical leadership.** A design others followed. A decision you argued for and won — or lost, and what happened next. A convention the team adopted. An engineer you onboarded who then shipped.
- **Cross-boundary work.** Anything that required agreement from a team you do not sit in is the earliest credible signal of the senior rung.

### 3.3 The mispositioning: the task list

The mid-level document that fails reads as a job description rather than a record: a list of responsibilities, phrased in the passive, describing what the employer assigned. It is not wrong, it is inert — it tells the reviewer what the role was and nothing about the person in it.

The diagnostic is one question, applied bullet by bullet: **who decided this?** If the honest answer for every bullet is "somebody else", the document says you execute other people's decisions well, which is exactly the claim a mid-level candidate is trying to move past.

```text
Before: Responsible for maintaining the checkout service and fixing bugs raised by QA.
After:  Owned checkout end to end for two years, from the payment-provider
        integration to the on-call rota; cut checkout errors from 2.1 to 0.4 per
        thousand orders by replacing a retry loop that double-charged on timeout.
```

The rewrite adds no seniority the candidate did not have. It reports the same two years accurately, and it names a decision, a mechanism and a measured consequence. Where you have no numbers at all, the substitutes — scale, scope, frequency, breadth, counterfactual — are in [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](quantifying-without-numbers.md).

## 4. Senior

This is where the mispositioning is most common and most costly, so this section is the longest one here.

### 4.1 The three things a senior document must demonstrate

| Signal | What it looks like on the page | What does not count |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | You identified the problem, framed it, sequenced it and shipped it, and the bullet says so | "Led the implementation of" a thing somebody else scoped |
| **Technical judgement at scale** | A choice, its rejected alternative, the constraint that decided it, the consequence | A list of technologies used, however impressive the list |
| **Raising the level around you** | Mentorship with a named outcome, a standard that outlived you, a review or design practice others adopted | "Mentored junior engineers" |

Judgement at scale is specifically judgement about **trade-offs**, not familiarity with tools. Consistency against availability. Latency against cost. Build against buy. The migration you sequenced in three stages instead of one because the rollback path mattered more than the calendar. The thing you deliberately did not build. A senior engineer is hired for the decisions they will make when nobody is watching, and the only proxy a resume can offer is decisions they already made.

### 4.2 The document tells a story of escalating responsibility

Read your entries oldest to newest and check that the scope grows. The most common structural defect in a senior resume is that the 2019 bullets and the 2025 bullets describe work of the same size — five roles, one altitude. A reviewer reading that sees someone who has repeated one year of experience several times, which is precisely the inference a senior candidate cannot afford.

Concretely: the earliest role gets one or two bullets at feature scale, the most recent gets four or five at system-and-decision scale, and somewhere in the middle the subject of the sentences shifts from "built" to "decided". The trajectory is the argument. If the trajectory is not there in the facts, the fix is not on the resume — it is finding the projects where you did decide, and there are usually more of them than you remember. That is the case for keeping [patterns/brag-document.md](brag-document.md).

### 4.3 The mispositioning that costs the most

**A senior engineer who documents their work as implementation reads exactly like a strong mid-level candidate.** Not a weak senior candidate — a strong mid-level one, which is a different requisition at a different band.

The mechanism is simple and unforgiving. Implementation done well is what a mid-level engineer is *for*. Describing it well therefore proves the mid-level bar, and stops. Nothing in a bullet that names only what was built lets a reviewer distinguish "was handed this and executed it cleanly" from "concluded this was the right thing to build, against opposition, and then executed it cleanly". Both people write the same sentence. Only one of them gets levelled correctly, and it is the one who wrote a different sentence.

Same project, both bullets true:

```text
Before: Migrated the monolith's order module into a separate Kotlin service with
        its own Postgres instance and a Kafka topic for order events.
After:  Split orders out of the monolith to unblock two teams that were serialising
        on a single release train; kept a shared database read path for one quarter
        rather than accept a dual-write, then cut over once the backfill verified.
        Releases went from fortnightly to daily, with no order lost in the cutover.
```

The second version names four things the first one does not: the **constraint** (two teams sharing a release train), the **decision** (split, with a deliberately temporary shared read path), the **trade-off accepted** (short-term coupling in exchange for avoiding dual-write risk), and the **consequence measured** (release frequency, zero loss). The first version could have been written by anyone who worked on the migration, including the contractor who wrote the Kafka producer.

Two more, because the pattern generalises past migrations:

```text
Before: Evaluated three message brokers and implemented the chosen one.
After:  Argued against adding a second message broker and partitioned the existing
        topic instead; the alternative added an operational surface a four-person
        team could not carry on call. Wrote down the traffic threshold at which the
        decision should be revisited.
```

```text
Before: Mentored junior engineers and helped with onboarding.
After:  Ran backend onboarding for six engineers over two years; two were promoted
        to mid-level inside that window, and time to first production change for a
        new joiner fell from three weeks to four days after I rewrote the local
        environment setup.
```

The second of these is the one people resist writing, because promotions of the people you mentored feel like their achievement rather than yours. It is the strongest evidence available that you raise the level around you, and the reviewer cannot infer it from the word "mentored".

Note also that the middle example is a decision **not** to build. Those are disproportionately senior and disproportionately absent from resumes, because there is no artefact to point at. Point at the reasoning instead.

### 4.4 The one-question test

For each bullet in your two most recent roles, ask: **could a strong mid-level engineer have written this sentence about the same project?** If yes, the bullet is not carrying your level, and you rewrite it or cut it.

Apply the test hardest to the first three bullets of the most recent role, because those are what actually gets read:

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, each timed covertly via session-recording software, 2023):** recruiters spent 17 seconds on a short one-page resume, 27 seconds on a mid-length one and 46 seconds on a long one — with the fastest observed pass at 12 seconds.

Twenty-seven seconds does not reach your fourth role. Whatever sets your band has to be above the fold of your most recent entry.

### 4.5 Standards, not just systems

The third signal — raising the level of the team — has a form that is easy to miss because it produces documents rather than software: the design-doc template that the team still uses, the on-call policy you wrote after the incident, the review standard, the SLO regime, the migration playbook two other teams reused. Name the artefact and name its reach. "Wrote the incident review process now used by four backend teams" is a stronger senior signal than most architecture bullets, and it costs one line.

## 5. Staff and principal

### 5.1 What the reviewer is testing

Three things, all of which sit outside your own team:

- **Influence beyond the team**, usually without authority over the people you influenced.
- **Decisions at organisational level** — technical strategy, platform direction, what the company stops doing.
- **Operating in genuine ambiguity**: you were given a problem that had not been defined, and defining it was the work.

The last of these is the real dividing line. A senior engineer is given a problem and solves it. A staff engineer is given a symptom, or a budget, or an argument between two teams, and produces the problem statement everyone then agrees to work on.

```text
Before: Led the platform team's migration to Kubernetes across three services.
After:  Asked to find out why deploys were slow; established that the constraint was
        four teams each maintaining a bespoke deploy path, not the deploy tooling.
        Wrote the consolidation proposal, carried it through two reviews I did not
        chair, and handed the rollout to the platform team. Deploy lead time across
        the four teams went from days to under an hour.
```

Two details in the rewrite carry the rung. The problem was reframed before it was solved. And the rollout was handed to a team the writer did not manage — influence without ownership, which is the thing that cannot be faked in an interview and is therefore worth stating precisely.

### 5.2 What counts as evidence

- **Named breadth.** "Adopted by six teams across two organisations" — a count of teams, not a count of services.
- **The problem you framed**, stated as a reframing, as above.
- **What you killed.** The project you argued should stop, the system you deprecated, the vendor you removed. Deletion at organisational scale is a staff-level act and almost nobody writes it down.
- **Writing that travelled.** An RFC that changed a decision, a standard adopted without a mandate, a platform other teams chose to use.
- **What changed for people who never met you.** The strongest staff bullets have beneficiaries the writer does not know by name.

Where a specific employer publishes its levelling rubric — several large ones do — the mapping between these signals and that company's language belongs to [patterns/company-tailoring.md](company-tailoring.md).

### 5.3 Depth in fewer technologies is more compelling than superficial knowledge of many

Take that as the governing rule of the staff-level document, and apply it to the skills block first.

A thirty-item technology inventory is not a stronger claim than a six-item one; it is a weaker one, for three reasons that compound. It is unfalsifiable, so it carries no information. It gets probed at its weakest item, and at this level the interviewer deliberately picks the hardest thing on the list. And by the mechanism in section 2.5, a floating list item acquires no duration and no recency, so a long list of bare strings dilutes the handful of skills that appear inside dated roles and are therefore genuinely attributed.

The staff-level skills block is short, ordered by depth, and every item in it appears again inside a dated entry. If a technology only ever appeared once, in one quarter, four years ago, it is not part of your claim.

The same principle governs the rest of the document. Fewer entries, longer entries. Fewer bullets per role, each one load-bearing. Roles older than roughly fifteen years compress to a single line each or disappear; nothing at this rung is decided by what you did in 2008.

### 5.4 Education shrinks to a line

One line: institution, degree, graduation year. No coursework, no honours class, no societies, no thesis title. At this rung the degree corroborates, it does not evidence. The one exception is a doctorate directly relevant to the role's technical domain, which stays one line but earns its place near the top.

### 5.5 The mispositioning: a longer senior resume

Two forms, and both are common enough to have a shape.

**The first** is the senior document with more entries on it. Same altitude, same implementation voice, more of it. It reads as an experienced senior engineer — a real and respectable hire, and a lower band than the one the candidate applied for. The tell: the largest noun in the document is a system rather than an organisation.

**The second** is the inventory. Faced with a staff job description covering strategy, platform, mentorship and cross-team work, the candidate tries to cover all of it by listing everything, and produces three pages that concentrate on nothing. Reviewers read this as breadth without depth even when the depth is genuinely there, because nothing on the page had room to be demonstrated.

The corrective for both is subtraction. Pick the three largest things you have done, give them the space, and delete whatever competes with them.

## 6. Engineering manager

### 6.1 A genuinely different document

Not a senior engineer's resume with "managed the team" appended. The manager document balances two claims that pull against each other: **technical credibility** — you can evaluate the work, hold a bar, and be trusted by engineers — and **people outcomes**, which is what you are actually paid for. Both must be present, and the ratio is roughly one part technical to three parts outcome.

### 6.2 The metrics are different in kind

An engineering manager's numbers are not bigger engineering numbers. They are a separate catalogue, and stating them is what makes the document a manager's document.

| Category | State | Example phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Team size and shape | Headcount, reporting lines, squads, contractors | "11 engineers across two squads, including 3 contractors" |
| Growth | What you hired, over what period, against what plan | "Grew the platform group from 4 to 11 over eighteen months" |
| Retention | Regretted attrition, honestly scoped to a period | "One regretted departure in two years" |
| Promotions of reports | How many, to what level — the strongest single manager metric | "Promoted 3: two to senior, one to staff" |
| Delivery cadence | Release frequency, lead time, predictability, incident rate | "Billing releases from monthly to weekly; change failure rate halved" |
| Cost | Budget owned, spend reduced, vendor decisions | "Cut observability spend by a third by consolidating two vendors" |
| Scope | Products or services owned, on-call ownership, stakeholders | "Owned billing and identity, and the shared on-call for both" |

Promotions of reports is the metric to lead with if you have it. It is externally validated — someone else's committee agreed — it is hard to fabricate, and it is the closest thing to a direct measurement of whether people grew under you.

Retention needs care. State it as a count over a stated period, never as a percentage you cannot reconstruct, and never in a way that implies you controlled attrition you did not. A manager who claims zero attrition across a growing team invites the question of who left and why.

### 6.3 How much technical content, and where

Keep one role's worth of hands-on depth, compressed, in the earlier entries — enough that a reader can see the engineering career the management career grew out of. In the current role, the technical content is not code; it is the technical decisions you own: architecture review, build-versus-buy calls, the technical bar in hiring, the incident-response standard.

Do not delete the IC history and do not let it run to three roles of bullets. It is corroboration, like education at staff level.

### 6.4 The mispositioning, in both directions

```text
Too technical:  Refactored the Terraform modules and reviewed most of the team's
                pull requests.
Too abstract:   Drove alignment across stakeholders and fostered a culture of
                psychological safety and continuous improvement.
Right:          Managed 11 engineers across two squads owning billing and identity;
                hired 5, promoted 3 (two to senior, one to staff) and held regretted
                attrition to one person over two years, while moving billing from
                monthly to weekly releases.
```

The first reads as someone who has not let go of the work, which raises the specific worry that the team's best engineer is being second-guessed by their manager. The second is worse in a different way: with no technical or numeric content at all, there is nothing to check, and the reviewer's default inference is that the candidate cannot evaluate engineering work. Unfalsifiable manager language is the single most discounted register on any resume — see [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md) for the phrases that trigger it.

### 6.5 If you are open to both IC and manager roles

Write two documents and decide per application which one goes out. A single hedged document that gives equal weight to both reads as someone who wants neither strongly, and it loses to a candidate who committed. This is a different axis from the parser-safe and designed pair in [patterns/two-document-strategy.md](two-document-strategy.md) — that pattern is about format, this is about the claim — but the discipline is the same: keep the facts in one place, and never let the two versions disagree about a number.

## 7. The signal that matters at every rung: legible progression

A recruiter reads promotion history as one of the strongest predictors available to them, and the reason is structural rather than statistical. Every other claim on your resume is your own account of your own work. A promotion is a judgement made about you by people who worked with you, over a period, with information no document can carry. It is the only externally validated line on the page.

So a document that makes progression legible is doing something no bullet can do — and the format decides whether it is legible at all.

**Split each title into its own dated entry under the employer.** Never merge several years and several titles into one block with a single date range.

```text
Acme S.p.A. — Milan, Italy
  Senior Backend Engineer                          Mar 2023 – Present
    · …
  Backend Engineer                                 Jun 2020 – Mar 2023
    · …
```

Two reasons, one human and one mechanical. The human one: a merged block states your current title and silently discards the fact that you earned it there, which converts your strongest validated claim into no claim at all. The mechanical one is documented — a stack of roles under one employer without individual date ranges is what produces "multiple current positions at the same employer" as a parser data issue, and duplicated employment blocks trigger a major finding of their own. The exact codes and the entry layout are in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md) section 8.2.

It also lands in the part of the page that gets looked at first:

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** the "recruiters spend six seconds on a resume" figure comes from a 2012 vendor eye-tracking exercise with roughly thirty participants, and it is superseded on duration by the covertly timed 2023 study in section 4.4. Its one durable observation — that roughly 80% of initial attention falls on the name, the current title and company, the previous title and company, the dates and the education — has never been independently replicated either. It is worth knowing only because it describes exactly the fields a promotion sequence occupies.

### 7.1 If you were never promoted

Long tenure at one title is read as a plateau unless the document says otherwise, so say otherwise, with evidence rather than adjectives. Show scope growth inside the single title: what you owned when you started, what you owned by the end, and the step in between.

```text
Backend Engineer, Acme S.p.A.                       Jun 2020 – Present
  · 2020–2022: owned the notifications service (one team, internal consumers)
  · 2022–2024: took over billing after a reorganisation, including the on-call rota
  · 2024–present: technical lead for the payments group's three services
```

That is a promotion sequence in everything but title, and it is honest. Flat titles are also a market artefact — many companies have two engineering titles in total — and stating the scope lets the reviewer level you on what you did rather than on your employer's naming conventions.

### 7.2 The reverse problem: a title larger than its scope

"Head of Engineering" at a five-person company will be calibrated against a scoped ladder and found short, and the candidate is often surprised. The remedy is the same instrument used in the opposite direction: put the scope numbers next to the title, so the reviewer levels you on evidence instead of on a word they distrust. "Head of Engineering (team of 4, one product, full P&L for infrastructure)" is a stronger claim than the bare title, because it stops the reviewer from guessing — and their guess is usually less flattering than the truth.

## 8. Downlevelling, and where it is actually decided

### 8.1 The mechanism

Downlevelling is being interviewed at a lower band than the one you applied for. It matters more than most candidates realise because **the band is usually fixed before the interview**: the requisition is opened at a level, the recruiter screens against that level, and the loop is calibrated to it. By the time you are demonstrating senior judgement in a system design round, the interviewers are scoring you against a mid-level rubric, and doing well against that rubric produces a mid-level offer.

An under-positioned document does not lose you the interview. It gets you the interview for a different job.

### 8.2 Under-positioning is more common than over-positioning

Among competent engineers it is not close. The habits that make someone good at mid-level work — describing what was built, crediting the team, declining to claim decisions that were partly someone else's — are exactly the habits that produce a mid-level document. The people most at risk are the ones who were promoted internally on the strength of work their manager could see directly, and who have therefore never had to write the decisions down.

### 8.3 The remedy is positional, not lexical

Seniority claims scattered through the document do nothing. No reviewer reads "spearheaded" as more senior than "built", and a summary that asserts "seasoned technical leader" is discounted on sight because it is unfalsifiable. What sets the band is the top of the document, in this order:

1. **The title line.** The market-standard title for the level you are applying to, honestly held. A creative internal title — `Member of Technical Staff III` — is levelled by a stranger who does not know your company's ladder, and they will guess low.
2. **The headline.** Years, domain, and the largest scope you have genuinely owned. Not adjectives.
3. **The first three bullets of the most recent role.** These must be decision-shaped, at the largest scope you can defend.

The construction of all three belongs to [guides/03-the-top-third.md](../guides/03-the-top-third.md), and that is the document to read next if this section is your problem. What this one adds is the reason: the top third is not merely the most-read area, it is where the band is assigned.

### 8.4 The fields that fix your band before anyone reads the document

Two of them sit in the application form, not the resume, and they override everything above.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Workday Recruiting exposes twelve mapped eligibility questions available for disqualification, among them salary expectations. Answers generate calculated fields usable in Automatic Stage Routing condition rules, and can carry positive or negative scores.

A salary expectation stated below your band is a self-inflicted downlevel that no bullet can undo, because it is a structured field and your resume is prose. Answer against the posted range for the level you applied to.

The legal context moved recently and moved in your favour:

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** the EU Pay Transparency Directive, with a transposition deadline of 7 June 2026, requires employers to disclose the initial pay level or range in the posting or before the interview, and forbids asking candidates about current or past pay. "What are your salary expectations" remains legal and remains a live knockout field.

So in the EU you can usually see the band before you name a number, and you cannot be asked what you currently earn. Use the first and decline to volunteer the second.

The third field is mechanical and nastier, because it downlevels you silently: the computed **years of experience**, which 44.0% of surveyed recruiters filter on, is derived from your parsed date ranges. A missing end date, a stacked-title block, or a stale file whose "Present" resolves against the document's own creation date will shorten your career by years without telling you. The mechanism and the fix are in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md) section 5.

### 8.5 The boundary: position at the top of your band, never above it

Positioning up is not the same as claiming up, and the difference is testable. Everything in section 8.3 is a re-selection and re-ordering of things you actually did. Nothing in it requires a fact that is not true.

The test to apply before promoting any project into your top three: **can you name the decision, the alternative you rejected, the constraint that decided it, and the consequence?** If you cannot, you were not the senior on that project, and putting it first will fail in the first technical conversation. Find a project where you can. Most people have one they had discounted because it was small — small and decided beats large and executed.

If you are downlevelled anyway, ask the recruiter which signals were missing for the higher band. The answer is free, it is usually specific, and it is information about your document rather than about you.

## 9. The five-minute calibration pass

Run this before sending, at any rung.

1. **Cover your name and hand the document to someone.** Ask which level they would interview it at. Do not tell them the answer first. This is the only reliable test in the list.
2. **Read only the first three bullets of your most recent role.** That is your band. Everything below is corroboration.
3. **Count the decisions.** How many bullets name a choice with a rejected alternative? At senior, this should be most of the recent ones; at mid, at least one per role.
4. **Check the gradient.** Is the scope in your earliest entry visibly smaller than in your latest? If not, the document reads as one year repeated.
5. **Interrogate the skills block.** Could you take the hardest possible question on every item? Delete the ones where the answer is no.
6. **Check the titles.** Does every promotion have its own dated line under the employer?
7. **Check the largest noun.** At mid it should be a service, at senior a system, at staff an organisation, at manager a team. If yours is a ticket, the level is wrong.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I have twelve years of experience but I keep getting mid-level offers" | Your bullets describe implementation, which is the mid-level bar done well. Years are not a signal; decisions are. |
| "I added 'senior', 'led' and 'spearheaded' throughout" | Adjectives and verbs do not carry level. The band is set by the title line, the headline and the first three bullets of the most recent role. |
| "I listed every technology I've touched so I match more searches" | You diluted the ones you can defend, and at staff level the interviewer picks the weakest item on the list deliberately. |
| "My resume is two pages because I'm senior" | Length follows content, not rank. A two-page document at one altitude still reads at that altitude. |
| "I merged my three titles at one company into a single entry" | You deleted the only externally validated claim on your resume, and created a parser data issue while doing it. |
| "I built a clone of a well-known app to show that I can code" | It shows you can follow instructions. There are no decisions in it, so the interview question about it has no answer. |
| "My internship bullet says I attended stand-ups and shadowed engineers" | That is attendance, not work. Say what shipped and who used it, even if it was small. |
| "I'm a manager now, so I took all the technical detail out" | With nothing checkable on the page, the reviewer's default inference is that you cannot evaluate engineering work. |
| "I want to keep options open between IC and manager, so one resume covers both" | It reads as wanting neither. Two documents, one decision per application. |
| "I gave my salary expectation from my last job because the form asked" | Salary expectation is a mapped eligibility field with routing rules attached. A number below your band downlevels you before anyone opens the file. |
| "I'm staff level, so I made the resume three pages" | Staff positioning is subtraction. Three of the largest things, given room, beat everything you have ever done. |
| "I've had the same title for six years, so there's nothing to show" | Show the scope growth inside the title with dated sub-entries. Flat titles are often the employer's convention, not your ceiling. |
