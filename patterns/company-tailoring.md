# Tailoring to a Specific Employer

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global, us, uk, eu** | **ATS: vendor-neutral**

**Read when:** tailoring for a named employer · whether to quote their values · what to change after reading the careers page · whether a different resume per company is worth it · how to do this at volume · whether they notice mimicry

**Not this file:** [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](../guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md) — if the question is how to read a job description and pick terms out of it, that is the file. This one is about reading the *employer*: the rubric behind the posting, published on their own site, which the posting is only a partial expression of.

## TL;DR

- **Large employers publish their evaluation rubrics, and their interviewers are trained to score against them.** Speaking in the rubric's terms is not flattery. It is answering the question you will actually be asked, in the vocabulary it will be asked in.
- **Tailoring changes emphasis, ordering and vocabulary. It never changes a fact.** If tailoring requires you to assert something you would not assert to a different employer, you are not tailoring; you are misrepresenting.
- **Never quote the values page back at them.** A document that recites a company's principles reads as research, not as fit — and recruiters read those exact strings hundreds of times a week. Supply the evidence; let the interviewer file it under the principle.
- **Your resume selects your interview questions.** At an employer with a published rubric, the interviewer picks which stories to probe from the document in front of them. Put the stories you want to be asked about on the page.
- **Tier it.** Full tailoring for a small number of roles you genuinely want, ten-minute tailoring for a middle band, and an untailored base document for volume — with volume acknowledged as the low-yield channel it is.
- **At tier one, spend half the budget on the document and half on finding a human inside the company.** The referral arithmetic beats the tailoring arithmetic and it is not close.

## 1. Why this is not flattery

Structured interviewing requires a fixed list of attributes and a scale to score them on, because that is what makes two interviewers' verdicts comparable. Any employer running structured interviews at scale therefore has the list written down. Once it is written down, publishing it costs nothing and buys employer branding, so a large employer usually does.

The consequence for you is mechanical. **The attributes on that published page are the headings on the feedback form your interviewer fills in afterwards.** A debrief is a conversation conducted in those words, in front of that form, often by people who were not in the room with you. The person arguing for you is arguing in the rubric's vocabulary, from notes they took while looking at your resume.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is why this is not sycophancy. Flattery is a claim about *them*: "I have long admired your commitment to customer centricity." Rubric tailoring is a claim about *you*, expressed in a shared vocabulary so it survives translation into the form. One is decoration. The other is the difference between an interviewer writing *strong ownership example, drove the migration end to end without a mandate* and writing *seems solid, hard to tell*.

**A calibration note on the evidence marker used below.** Everywhere else in this knowledge base, `Vendor-documented` means the company that builds the parser. In this document it means the employer publishing about its own hiring process, on its own careers site. That is the primary source for *what the employer says it assesses*. It is not evidence that the assessment happens as described, that interviewers apply it consistently, or that the rubric on the page is the rubric in the room this quarter. Treat it as the published contract, not as measured behaviour.

## 2. Where a rubric lives when there is one

| Source | What it yields | Worth |
|---|---|---|
| The "how we hire" page | The attribute list itself: the named things every interviewer scores. This is the rubric. | Highest. Read it before the posting. |
| The values or principles page | The vocabulary, and the employer's own ranking of what matters — the order on that page is rarely accidental. | High for word choice, dangerous to quote. |
| The company's interview-prep material | Which format the answers are expected in, and sometimes which attributes each round covers. | High and under-read. |
| The engineering or company blog | What they are proud of, and the in-house nouns for their own systems. | Medium. Best source of vocabulary. |
| The posting's own ordering | What this requisition's author weighs. The first three requirements are the priority; the last three are a wish list. | Medium — technique in [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](../guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md). |

**What is never public, and where people invent:** the scoring scale, the weights between attributes, the bar for each level, how a hiring committee resolves a split, and whether any of it is applied consistently across teams. Nobody outside knows these. Advice that claims to know them is worth nothing, and the deeper you push a document towards an imagined scoring function, the more you distort it away from being a true account of your work.

There is one specific invention worth naming, because it drives a great deal of wasted effort.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** that a large employer's software scans a resume for its published principles or values and scores the document against them. No employer documents this, no vendor documents principle-keyword scoring, and the published rubrics are described everywhere as *interview* assessment frameworks. The principles are read by people, in interviews, from your evidence. Writing the principle names into the document to feed a scanner feeds nothing and costs you with the human reader — section 8.

## 3. Worked case: the data-driven employer

Google publishes both halves: the attributes it hires against, and resume advice from its own recruiters.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Google's hiring pages name the attributes assessed across its interview process — general cognitive ability, leadership, role-related knowledge and experience, and "Googleyness", which the company defined publicly in December 2024 in terms including comfort with ambiguity, a bias to action, high standards and prizing feedback. Google's own resume guidance instructs candidates to state what was accomplished, as measured by a specific figure, by doing a specific thing.

Two published things, pointing the same way: **this employer's stated preference is for quantified reasoning, and it says so in the guidance it gives candidates.** That converts directly into document decisions.

- **Quantify everything, including the awkward ones.** Not the two impressive bullets and eleven prose ones. A document that is measured throughout is itself evidence of the mindset. The formula and the metric families are [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](../guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md); what changes here is the *proportion*, not the technique.
- **Foreground one project that began without a specification.** Ambiguity is invisible on a resume unless you name the starting condition. "Defined the problem" is worth a clause.
- **Show a short decision-to-shipped interval.** Bias to action is evidenced by a date span, not by an adjective.
- **Show resourcefulness as a constraint clause.** Done without the headcount, without the budget, without the vendor.

```text
Weak:   Led a data quality initiative for the analytics platform, improving
        reliability and stakeholder confidence in reporting

Better: Cut analytics pipeline incident volume from 11 to 2 a month by
        instrumenting freshness checks across 40 tables, scoped from an
        open-ended "reporting is untrustworthy" complaint with no spec, and
        shipped in six weeks with no additional headcount
```

Same project. The second version carries a magnitude, an undefined starting point, an interval and a constraint — the four things the published attributes ask about — and it never once uses the word Googleyness.

**The honest limit.** Two of the four attributes are assessed in conversation and cannot be scored from a document. What the resume can do is supply the material the interviewer draws on when they get there. That is the correct ambition for every case in this section.

## 4. Worked case: the short published value set

Meta publishes a short list of core values, revised in 2022: move fast, focus on long-term impact, build awesome things, live in the future, be direct and respect your colleagues, and "Meta, Metamates, Me" — the company as a shared endeavour rather than a set of individual careers.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** the list is published on Meta's own corporate site as the company's values, each with a short gloss. It is a values statement rather than a scored interview rubric, which makes it a weaker instrument than a principles list — read it as vocabulary and emphasis, not as a checklist.

A short list is easier to work with than a long one, because each value maps onto a bullet *type*:

| Value | The bullet that evidences it |
|---|---|
| Move fast | A short cycle from prototype to shipped, with a measured result at the end. |
| Focus on long-term impact | The thing that is still running, the migration whose payoff arrived a year later, the deprecation that removed a class of incident permanently. |
| Build awesome things | Scope and scale: the size of the surface you owned, the population it served. |
| Live in the future | Adopting a technology or a working practice before it was standard, with the outcome that followed. |
| Be direct and respect your colleagues | **Not evidencable on a resume.** Do not try. |
| Meta, Metamates, Me | Work you carried that was not your team's remit, or a handover that made someone else's problem go away. |

**Move fast is the one worth building the document around**, because it is the only value on the list that a resume can prove rather than assert. A date span plus a shipped outcome plus a measurement is a fact; "I move fast" is an adjective, and adjectives are discounted by human skimmers and ranking models alike.

```text
Prototyped an in-app referral flow in 2 weeks, shipped to 100% of users in
6, and retired the vendor tool it replaced the same quarter — referral
signups up 34% against the vendor baseline
```

Note what makes that bullet work: it is three dates and two numbers. It would be an equally good bullet at an employer that has never published a value in its life. That is the test a tailored bullet should pass.

## 5. Worked case: the long principles list

Amazon publishes sixteen leadership principles, with customer obsession first and ownership second, and — unusually for a large employer — frugality among them. Two were added in 2021, which is worth knowing before you tailor to a page you last read some years ago.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Amazon publishes the principles on its own jobs site, tells candidates in its own interview-preparation material to arrive with specific examples structured as situation, task, action and result, and describes the Bar Raiser as a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team who participates in the decision. The principles are stated as the framework interviewers assess against.

This is the strongest form of the case, because **the published document is not an aspiration, it is the interview rubric**. Each interviewer is looking for evidence against particular principles and writes feedback in those terms. Which produces the single most useful reframe in this document, and it generalises well beyond one employer:

**Your resume is a menu of interview questions.** The interviewer picks the stories to probe from what is on the page. A document with no cost story on it will not produce a frugality question that you answer well; it will produce a frugality question that you answer from nothing, in the room, under time pressure.

So the two moves are concrete:

1. **Put a cost number on the resume.** Most engineers never do. Infrastructure spend reduced, a vendor contract retired, cost per transaction, a job that ran on two people instead of five. Frugality is load-bearing here in a way it is nowhere else, and a single number pre-answers an entire interview slot.
2. **Put one unambiguous ownership story on the resume.** Something you carried past the boundary of your role, kept after the interesting part was over, or fixed when it was not assigned to you. Ownership is second on the published list for a reason.

```text
Weak:   Contributed to a cost optimisation programme for the data platform

Better: Cut data platform spend from $84k to $31k a month by moving cold
        partitions to object storage and killing 3 unused pipelines —
        proposed it, ran the migration, and owned the on-call for it after
```

That single bullet carries a cost outcome and an ownership arc, which pre-answers two interview slots. **What it does not do is name either principle.** Labelling your bullets with the employer's principle names is the failure described in section 8, and at this employer specifically it is the most common one.

## 6. The method for the employer that publishes nothing

Most employers are not in section 3, 4 or 5. They have a careers page with three adjectives on it and no rubric at all. The method still works; it just draws on more sources and yields less certainty.

**Five reads, in this order. Twenty minutes total.**

1. **The careers page's stated values.** Even three adjectives tell you which words they use for good work. Note the nouns, ignore the adjectives.
2. **The engineering or company blog.** What they choose to write about is what they are proud of, and the post titles give you their in-house names for their own systems. This is the best vocabulary source in existence and almost nobody reads it.
3. **The posting's ordering.** First requirement is the priority. A requirement repeated in both the responsibilities and the requirements sections is the actual job.
4. **Recent public announcements.** A funding round, a launch, a market entry, a reorg, an earnings call. This tells you what the team is being hired *for*, which is frequently not what the posting says. A posting is often months older than the plan.
5. **Profiles of three or four people already in the target team.** What their previous titles were, what kind of company they came from, how long they stayed. This shows you the path in — which of your projects reads as "the same kind of person" to whoever hires there.

**Then three moves, and only three.**

| Move | The decision | The test |
|---|---|---|
| **Which project leads** | Which of your projects is closest to the problem they are visibly working on, promoted to the top of its role and given the most bullets. | Would a reader who skims only the first role know you have done this before? |
| **Which vocabulary** | Their noun for the same object, wherever both terms are legitimate for what you did. | Could you defend the term for ten minutes with the person who built it? |
| **Which outcome leads** | The same project has several true measurements. Lead with the one this employer buys. | Is the number you promoted actually true and actually yours? |

The third move is the one people miss, so here it is worked. One project, three employers, three honest framings:

```text
Payments employer:
  Reduced authorisation failures on checkout from 4.1% to 1.6% by adding
  retry routing across three acquirers

Infrastructure employer:
  Cut p99 checkout latency from 2.4s to 700ms by replacing a synchronous
  fan-out with a queued settlement path

Consumer growth employer:
  Lifted completed checkouts 9% by removing two steps and rebuilding the
  service behind them
```

Same rebuild, same quarter, same person, three true sentences. Nothing was invented and no fact moved. What changed is which measurement got the first four words of the line, which is the position a skimming reader actually reads.

> **Study (n=114 experienced recruiters, covertly timed via session recording, 2023):** recruiters spent 17 seconds on a short one-page resume, 27 on a mid-length one, 46 on a long one. That budget is the entire justification for the ordering moves above: ordering decides what gets read at all.

### 6.1 When there is no blog and no values page

Most employers are not tech companies, and the five reads collapse to two or three. What substitutes, in descending order of usefulness:

- **The product itself.** Sign up, use it, and find one specific thing about it. A candidate who has used the product is rarer than a candidate who has read the values page, and the specificity is not fakeable.
- **The founders' or leadership's public writing and talks.** At a company under a few hundred people this is the values page, and it is usually more honest than one.
- **The customers or case-studies page.** It tells you who they sell to, which tells you what "good" means to them — a team selling into banks values reliability and audit trails; a team selling to consumers values shipping cadence.
- **The recruiter's own language in the posting.** At a small employer the posting is often written by the hiring manager. Its vocabulary is theirs personally, not a corporate template, and matching it is unusually effective.
- **Who they have hired recently, from their own announcements or team page.** The path in, same as move five above.

**Where the trail runs out, stop.** An employer that publishes nothing gets tier-2 treatment on the strength of the posting alone. Manufacturing certainty about a company's values from a homepage is how mimicry starts.

### 6.2 The one place where the rubric is literally scored on paper

Big tech publishes a rubric that people interview against. The public sector publishes a rubric that people **mark your written application against**, which is a stronger case than anything in sections 3 to 5.

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** the UK Civil Service publishes Success Profiles, naming the elements assessed — behaviours, strengths, ability, experience and technical skills — with a published list of named behaviours and level-by-level descriptions of what each looks like. Applications are commonly submitted as word-limited statements against specific named behaviours and are scored against those descriptions. Comparable published competency frameworks govern EU institution recruitment and many national and regional public bodies.

Where a framework like this applies, everything in this document inverts. The named competencies are not vocabulary to absorb quietly; they are **headings you are expected to answer under, explicitly, one at a time**, usually in a structured form rather than in a resume at all. Quoting the framework's own words back is correct here, and omitting them costs you marks. The mimicry warning in section 8 does not apply to a form that asks you to demonstrate a named behaviour in 250 words.

Know which regime you are in before you write. Applying to a scored competency form as though it were a tech resume loses points that were freely available; applying to a tech employer as though its principles were a scored form produces exactly the recitation that section 8 warns about.

## 7. What tailoring changes, and what it never changes

**Emphasis, ordering and vocabulary. Nothing else.**

| Changes | Never changes |
|---|---|
| Which project appears first, and how many bullets it gets | Employer names, dates, titles, degrees |
| Which of several true measurements leads the bullet | Any number you have quoted anywhere |
| Which legitimate synonym you use for the same object | The scope of what you actually did |
| Which skills sit first in the skills block | Which skills are in it at all |
| Which two sentences the top block spends itself on | Whether a claim is true |

**The test, and it is a single sentence: would you write this line on the version you send to their closest competitor?** If the answer is no because the emphasis would be wrong there, you are tailoring correctly. If the answer is no because the line would not be *true* there, you have crossed into misrepresentation, and the line has to come out.

The four crossings, in order of how often they happen:

1. **Retitling to match their ladder.** Writing Staff Engineer because that is the rung they are hiring at. This is a factual change, it is the single most checkable thing on the document, and background verification checks exactly this. Write your real title and, where it is unhelpfully internal, put the market-standard title alongside it — the mechanics are in [references/parsing-rules.md](../references/parsing-rules.md).
2. **Inflating the verb.** `Led` for was-on-the-team-of, `Owned` for contributed-to. The verb is a claim about your authority and it is the first thing an interviewer probes. The register is [references/action-verbs.md](../references/action-verbs.md).
3. **Adopting their stack because you read the blog.** They use Rust, you have read about Rust, Rust appears in your skills block. Every entry in that block is a question you have invited.
4. **Adopting a value you do not hold.** Writing that you are obsessed with customers when you have never spoken to one. This is the crossing that feels harmless because it is about character rather than fact, and it is the one that collapses fastest in an interview, because the follow-up is always "tell me about a time".

A useful frame for all four: **tailoring reorders evidence you already had. If a tailoring move requires new evidence, it is not a tailoring move — it is a claim, and claims need to be true before they need to be well placed.**

## 8. The limit: mimicry, and how it is detected

This technique degrades badly when overdone, and the degradation is not gradual. It has three signatures.

**Recitation.** The values-page phrases quoted back, in the summary or the letter. The employer's own strings are the most recognisable text in your document precisely because they are published: a recruiter at that company reads them every day and has seen them pasted into applications hundreds of times. A document built out of them reads as *research*, not as *fit*, and research is the cheapest thing you can demonstrate. It signals that you read a page, not that you are the person on it.

**The mirror.** A document that matches the posting and the values page almost perfectly. This reads as generated, and in 2026 that suspicion is cheap to form because generation is cheap to do. The coverage guidance in [guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md](../guides/05-keywords-and-tailoring.md) — roughly three of every four hard requirements, then stop — exists for this reason and applies to rubrics as much as to postings.

**The stale rubric.** Published frameworks are revised: Meta rewrote its value set in 2022, Amazon added two principles in 2021, Google published a formal definition of Googleyness in December 2024. Tailoring precisely to a version that has been superseded is worse than not tailoring, because it demonstrates out-of-date research with confidence. Re-read the page in the week you apply.

**The rule that prevents all three: name the company's problem, not the company's virtues.** One accurate sentence about what they are actually working on — a system named on their blog, a market they just entered, a constraint their launch implies — outperforms a paragraph about their principles, because it is not available to someone who only skimmed the values page.

And there is a division of labour that keeps the resume clean:

- **The resume carries vocabulary, ordering and emphasis.** It should be readable by a competitor without embarrassment.
- **The cover letter carries the one company-specific sentence.** That is what a letter is for, and the evidence that tailoring works at all was measured on letters, not on resumes — see [guides/06-cover-letters.md](../guides/06-cover-letters.md).

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** that tailoring a resume to a company multiplies your callback rate by some named factor. No field experiment isolating resume tailoring has been located. The nearest real evidence measures cover letters, and it is in section 9. When a number is attached to resume tailoring specifically, it has been invented.

## 9. Tiering, because this cannot be done for every application

Full per-company tailoring — five reads, three moves, a rewritten top block, a tailored letter, a re-export and an extraction check — costs the better part of an hour. The volume the market now demands makes that arithmetic impossible.

> **Study (100M+ applications across 200,000+ jobs, 2021 to 2026):** applications per hire tripled since 2021 to more than 300, and the global median application-to-interview rate is 3.6 to 4.7%. The full conversion arithmetic, and why it implies one to two hundred targeted cold applications per offer, is in [guides/01-how-screening-actually-works.md](../guides/01-how-screening-actually-works.md).

An hour each across that volume is a hundred-plus hours of work. Nobody does it, and the people who try stop applying instead. So tier deliberately rather than by exhaustion:

| Tier | Which roles | Budget | What you do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — wanted** | Five to ten at a time. Roles you would accept on the spot. | 45–60 min each, plus the referral hunt | Everything in this document, plus a tailored letter, plus an attempt to reach a human inside the company. |
| **2 — plausible** | The middle band. Good fit, right level, no strong pull. | 10 min each | The posting workflow only: canonical title string, skills-block reorder, top block. No rubric read. |
| **3 — volume** | Everything else worth a click. | 0 min | One well-built base document per job family. Accurate, parser-clean, untailored. |

**Tier 3 is a real channel and a low-yield one, and it should be described that way rather than defended.** Its purpose is coverage and market information — which titles get replies, which requirements keep recurring — not conversion. It is worth doing because the marginal cost is near zero. It is not worth improving.

**The rule that makes tier 1 rational rather than romantic:** at tier 1, spend half the budget on the document and half on finding a person.

> **Study (54M+ applications across 93,000 jobs, January 2021 to March 2026):** referred candidates pass the initial screen at 52% against 35% for all candidates. Vendor platform data, so treat the magnitude as directional; the direction is corroborated by peer-reviewed work showing the referral advantage survives controlling for observable skill.

> **Study (7,287 applications to real openings, randomised three-arm field experiment, 2020):** callback rates were 10.7% with no cover letter, 12.5% with a generic one, and 16.4% with a tailored one. Vendor-run, unreplicated and predating the generated-application era, so read the direction rather than the magnitude — but it is the only randomised evidence located that tailoring pays at all, and it pays on the letter.

Put those two beside each other and the budget allocates itself. Tailoring moves a callback rate by a few points on a small base. A referral moves a first-screen pass rate by seventeen points on the same application. **Do both at tier 1, and if you can only do one, do the referral.**

**Two anti-patterns, both extremely common.** The first is spending tier-1 effort on tier-3 roles — people tailor hardest to the job they are least likely to get, because it is the most exciting. The second is running tier 3 on a tier-1 role because the deadline was today. Fix both by fixing a weekly hour budget in advance and letting tier 1 claim it first.

Finally: keep a one-line-per-company record — what their rubric emphasises, who you know there, which version you sent, on what date. It costs seconds and it is what makes the second application to the same employer cheap. The habit that supplies its raw material is [patterns/brag-document.md](brag-document.md).

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I put their core values in my summary" | The most recognisable strings in your document are theirs, not yours. It reads as having skimmed a page, which is the cheapest signal available and is not fit. |
| "I labelled my bullets with their leadership principle names" | The rubric is how the interviewer files your evidence, not a template for your document. Labelling does the filing badly and announces the tactic. |
| "I wrote Staff Engineer because that is the level they hire at" | A factual change, not an emphasis change, and it is the most verifiable line on the page. Tailoring never touches titles, dates or employers. |
| "Their blog said Rust so I added Rust to my skills" | Every entry in that block is an interview question you volunteered. A floating skill with no dated role attached is the first one asked. |
| "I wrote that I am customer-obsessed and move fast" | Unfalsifiable adjectives are discounted by human skimmers and ranking layers alike. The value gets evidenced by a date span and a number or not at all. |
| "I tailored to their values page, which I read a couple of years ago" | Published frameworks get revised. Confident research into a superseded version is worse than no research. Re-read the page the week you apply. |
| "I matched their posting and their rubric almost perfectly" | Near-perfect coverage reads as machine-generated in 2026. Cover roughly three of four hard requirements and stop. |
| "I spent an hour on each of forty applications and burned out" | Tier 1 is five to ten roles at a time, not forty. The rest get ten minutes or none, on purpose. |
| "I tailored my dream application beautifully and applied through the portal" | The cold portal is the weakest channel available. Half of a tier-1 budget belongs to finding a person, not to the document. |
| "There is no rubric published, so there is nothing to tailor to" | The blog, the posting's ordering, the recent announcements and the team's profiles give you all three moves. Section 6. |
| "I sent my normal resume to a public-sector competency application" | Where the framework is published and the form is marked against named behaviours, answering under those headings in their words is the task. You left available marks on the table. Section 6.2. |
