# The Brag Document

> **Last Updated: August 2026** | **Markets: global** | **ATS: vendor-neutral**

**Read when:** you cannot remember what you did last year · your resume reads like a job description · you improved something and lost the number · a review is coming and nothing is written down · you are leaving and about to lose your records

**Not this file:** [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](quantifying-without-numbers.md) — for the case where the numbers are already gone. This document is the habit that stops that case from recurring.

## TL;DR

- **Keep an achievement log. Every resume you write for the rest of your career is a query against it**, not a feat of memory.
- **Five fields per entry: what happened, the number and where the number came from, the constraint, who else was involved, the date.** Nothing else. A longer template is a template you abandon.
- **The source of the number is the field everybody omits and the only one you cannot reconstruct two years later.** A figure you cannot trace is a figure you cannot defend in an interview.
- **Weekly if you are early-career, monthly or quarterly if you are senior — and put it in the calendar.** An uncalendared habit does not survive a busy quarter.
- **Keep it somewhere that survives your leaving.** A record on an internal wiki is a record you are locked out of on your last day, usually within the hour.
- **The note does not have to be well written. It has to be accurate and dated.** Writing happens later, once, from the record.
- **It is not only a resume input.** The same entries are your self-review, your promotion case, your interview stories and your answer when someone asks what you do.

## 1. The failure this repairs

Somebody opens a resume that has not been touched in three years and finds that the three years are missing. Not the job titles — those are recoverable from an email signature — but everything that would make the document worth reading. The figure the latency dropped to. How many markets the rollout reached. How big the team was by the end. What made the work hard enough that finishing it meant something.

All of that existed at the time. It was on a dashboard, in a release note, in a thread where somebody said thank you. None of it was written down, and by the time it is needed it cannot be recovered: the dashboard's retention window has rolled over, the thread is in an account you no longer have, and the colleague who would remember has left.

What memory retains is the *shape* of a job rather than its results. So what gets written is a list of responsibilities — accurate, unfalsifiable, and almost identical to the job posting you were hired against. It is worth nothing to a reader choosing between you and forty other people who held the same title, because it contains no information that distinguishes you from any of them.

**This is the largest single cause of vague resumes, and it is not a writing problem.** No amount of rewriting adds a number that was never captured. Two of the practitioner sources behind this knowledge base, writing independently and for different audiences, identify exactly this failure and prescribe exactly the same remedy: record the outcome at the moment it happens, with the number attached, on a schedule.

### 1.1 Why the cost of not doing it went up

The habit was always sensible. What changed is how many times you now have to pay for its absence.

> **Study (100M+ applications across 200,000+ jobs, 2021 to 2026):** applications per hire have tripled since 2021 to more than 300, and a candidate is approximately 50% less likely to receive an interview than five years ago. The global median application-to-interview rate is 3.6 to 4.7%.

A funnel that narrow is not survived by sending one document more often. It is survived by sending a document tailored to each posting, which means writing more versions than anyone wrote five years ago. Tailoring is cheap when the evidence is already recorded and expensive when every application starts with an hour of trying to remember. **The habit is not really about writing a better resume. It is about lowering the marginal cost of the next one to minutes.**

### 1.2 What the evidence for this habit actually is

Be honest about the tier this sits in.

> **Folklore — no traceable source:** no field experiment measures the effect of keeping an achievement log on callbacks, promotions or pay. Nothing in this document is supported by a controlled trial, and the numbers that circulate about quantified achievements lifting callbacks are themselves untraceable — the tracing is in [references/evidence-and-myths.md](../references/evidence-and-myths.md).

The argument for the practice is mechanical rather than empirical, and it is strong on those terms: a number you recorded can go on the page and a number you did not record cannot. The alternative to the habit is not a different method, it is reconstruction from memory — and everything downstream of this KB's advice on bullets assumes evidence exists to write about. This is where it comes from.

## 2. The entry: five fields, deliberately small

The reason most achievement logs die is that the first entry took twenty minutes. Keep the unit small enough that the habit survives a bad week.

| Field | What goes in it | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| **What happened** | One line, plain language, no polish | The thing itself. If it needs a paragraph, it is two entries. |
| **The number, and its source** | The figure, the system it came from, the period, the baseline | The figure is the resume; the source is what lets you defend the figure. |
| **The constraint** | What made it hard, or why it might not have happened | This is the difference between a task and a story. |
| **Who else** | Names, and what each of them did | Keeps your share honest while you still remember it. |
| **The date** | Today's date, and the period the work covers | Decides which role on the resume this eventually belongs to. |

**The whole entry should take under two minutes.** Longer than that and you will skip it on the day something big happens, which is the day it matters most.

### 2.1 The source of the number is the field everybody omits

Two years later you will have a figure and no idea where it came from. You will not remember whether it was a p95 or a mean, whether it was measured over a week or a quarter, whether the comparison was against the previous month or the previous year, or whether the improvement was yours or coincided with a hardware refresh. At that point the number is unusable in the only place it matters — the moment an interviewer asks how you measured it.

Worse, a half-remembered number does not stay put. It gets rounded in a friendly direction each time it is retold, and by the third telling you are quoting a figure you can no longer support. That is indistinguishable from exaggeration and it is almost never deliberate.

So the field is not "the number". It is the number **and the four things that make it checkable**: the system it lives in, the metric, the window, and what it was compared against.

```text
Weak:   "made checkout a third faster"
Usable: "checkout p95 4.1s -> 1.2s. Grafana board 'checkout-latency',
         30-day windows either side of the 12 Mar deploy. Mean barely
         moved — the win was in the tail."
```

The second form is not better writing. It is the same claim with its evidence stapled to it, and it costs eight extra seconds on the day.

### 2.2 The constraint is what makes it a story

A metric on its own is a fact. A metric plus the reason it was difficult is an achievement, and it is the part interviewers actually probe. Nobody asks "what was the number"; they ask "what was hard about it".

Capture the release freeze you had to get an exception for, the owner who had left, the vendor who stopped replying, the two-week deadline you did not set, the migration that had to run without downtime because the business would not accept a maintenance window. None of that is in any dashboard, and it is entirely gone within a month.

### 2.3 Who else was involved

Write down the other people and what each of them did, at the time, while the answer is obvious.

Two payoffs. First, it makes the honest description easy to write later — "led", "built", "contributed to" and "supported" are four different claims, and choosing the right one from a two-year-old memory tends to produce the flattering one by default. An interviewer who asks a second question finds the seam immediately. Second, this field quietly becomes your reference list and your referral map: the people who saw the work are the people worth asking, and by the time you need them you will not remember who was in the room.

### 2.4 What not to put in it

- **Tasks with no outcome.** "Attended the architecture review" is a diary entry, not a record.
- **Anything you would not say out loud in the room.** The document's value is that you can quote it directly.
- **Confidential artefacts.** Record the ratio, the method and the period; never copy the contract, the customer list or the export. A screenshot of a commercial agreement sitting in your personal notes is a problem you have created for yourself, and the derived figure was all you needed anyway. Where an absolute number cannot leave the building, an order of magnitude usually can: "a seven-figure annual bill, cut by a third" discloses nothing and evidences everything.

## 3. Cadence

The interval is not a matter of taste. It follows from how large your meaningful units of work are.

| Stage | Cadence | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Early-career | **Weekly**, fifteen minutes | Items are smaller and more numerous, and a week is the longest window you can still recall in detail. Wait a month and you keep only the largest item. |
| Mid-level | **Monthly** | The unit is a project or a release rather than a task. |
| Senior, staff, management | **Monthly or quarterly** | The meaningful units — a migration, a reorganisation, a hire who worked out, a strategy that turned out right — resolve slowly. A weekly log at this level is a record of meetings. |

**Calendar it.** A recurring event with the file linked from the invitation. This sounds trivial and it is the whole mechanism: an uncalendared habit does not survive a quarter with a launch in it, and the quarters with launches in them are the ones worth recording.

**Capture at the moment, file on the schedule.** These are two different acts. When the dashboard shows the drop, when someone sends the thank-you, when the incident closes — write the two-minute note *then*, anywhere. The scheduled session is only for tidying those notes into entries and catching what you missed. A cadence with no in-the-moment capture degrades into a monthly attempt to remember the month.

### 3.1 The annual pass

Once a year, read the entire document.

- Fix entries that no longer make sense to a stranger. You are now that stranger.
- Delete the things that turned out not to matter. A log nobody prunes stops being readable, and an unreadable log gets abandoned.
- Pull the strongest handful into a shortlist at the top. Those are your career highlights, your promotion case and the opening of your next conversation.
- Check the numbers against their sources **while you still have access to the sources**.

Tie it to a fixed month so it cannot slip — the month before review season, the month of your work anniversary, whichever you will actually notice. A date with no anchor becomes December, and December never happens.

### 3.2 The exception that overrides the calendar

Do a full pass **the week you decide to leave, and again the week you hand in notice**. Access to dashboards, ticket systems, mail archives and the people who remember ends on your last day, sometimes at the moment you announce rather than the moment you go. Everything you did not verify before then is permanently an estimate.

## 4. Where to keep it

Be practical. A single markdown file, a note in whatever notes app you already have open, a spreadsheet with five columns. Anything that is one file, chronological and searchable. The format has never been the reason one of these fails.

There is exactly one hard constraint, and it is mundane enough that people ignore it and lose years of records:

**It must live somewhere that survives your leaving the employer.**

| Do not keep it in | What happens |
|---|---|
| The internal wiki | Access ends with your account, usually within the hour of your last day. |
| A company cloud drive or workspace account | Same, and the offboarding is automated so nobody can grant you an exception. |
| Work email, as messages to yourself | Same, plus it is unreadable as a record. |
| The work laptop only | You hand it back. Anything not synced elsewhere goes with it. |
| A ticket system, as comments | Not yours, not exportable, and gone the moment the employer changes tools. |

Keep it in a personal file, in a personal account, and if it lives in a cloud service keep a local copy. This costs nothing and it is the difference between a ten-year record and three separate three-year gaps.

Two smaller rules follow from the same logic:

- **A link to an internal dashboard is not a record.** It is a pointer that expires with your badge. Write the value into the note; keep the link as a convenience, not as the evidence.
- **One place, not four.** Notes scattered across a starred-messages list, a calendar, a paper notebook and two documents are not a record — they are four fragments none of which you will consult. If you already have fragments, the first annual pass is merging them.

## 5. What it feeds

This is the argument that makes the habit worth the fifteen minutes, and the reason to sell it to yourself as something other than resume admin. The record has at least six consumers, and the resume is only the most visible one.

| Consumer | What it takes from the record | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Resume, and each tailored version** | The entries whose numbers match what the posting says it cares about | Every application |
| **Self-review or performance review** | Everything in the period, with constraints attached | Each cycle |
| **Promotion case** | The entries showing scope growing over time, and the ones with other people's names in them | When the case is built, usually with no notice |
| **Interview stories** | The constraint and the decision, more than the number | Every interview loop |
| **The thirty-second answer** | One entry, the strongest, said out loud | Any time someone asks what you do |
| **Salary and renewal conversations** | The money and scale entries, with sources | Whenever the conversation happens |

**The correct framing is that the resume is downstream of this record rather than a separate artefact.** You do not write a resume from scratch. You query a record you have been keeping, select against a specific job description, and format the result. That is a two-hour job instead of a two-week one, and it is the reason the tailoring advice elsewhere in this KB is realistic rather than aspirational.

It also fixes a problem that has nothing to do with job hunting. [patterns/two-document-strategy.md](two-document-strategy.md) describes the way two versions of a resume silently disagree about the same figure over months. The fix is a single source of truth for every number, held outside both documents. This is that source. If you keep one of these, you already have the fix.

## 6. The self-review, when nobody asks you for one

Plenty of people work somewhere with no performance process at all: small companies, contracting, agency work, a manager whose idea of a review is a fifteen-minute conversation with no document. The habit above still works, but it is missing the thing a formal cycle provides, which is a forced moment of reflection at a fixed interval.

Run it yourself. Once or twice a year, in an hour, write a structured reflection on three questions: **what you did, how you did it, and what changed as a result.**

```text
Period: January to June 2026

What I did
  - four to six items, each with its number and the source of the number
  - one line each, taken straight from the log

How I did it
  - the constraint on each, and the decision I made because of it
  - what I chose not to do, and why

What changed as a result
  - for the product or the customer
  - for the team, and for the people I worked with
  - for what I can now do that I could not do in January

What I would do differently
  - one item, honestly stated
```

Three rules make this work:

**One: frame it as evidence collection, not self-promotion.** This matters more than it sounds, because the people who most need the habit are precisely the ones who find self-promotion uncomfortable and therefore never start. You are not arguing that you are good. You are writing down what happened while you can still verify it. At the moment of writing the document has an audience of one — you — and whether any of it is ever shown to anyone is a separate decision made later.

**Two: do not grade yourself.** No scores, no ratings, no "exceeded expectations". Grading invites either inflation or the reflexive undercount, and in twelve months neither tells you anything. Record what happened and let the evidence carry the judgement.

**Three: write it in plain past tense, first person, and keep it.** It becomes the raw material for the next promotion conversation, and — read back three years later — the clearest picture you will ever get of whether your scope is actually growing. That question is the subject of [patterns/seniority-ladder.md](seniority-ladder.md), and this document is how you answer it about yourself with evidence instead of a feeling.

## 7. Turning an entry into a bullet

The conversion is mechanical, because the entry already holds the elements the bullet formula needs. What follows is one worked example, end to end.

**The note, written the same afternoon, in ninety seconds:**

```text
2026-03-14
checkout timeouts finally fixed. p95 was 4.1s, now 1.2s.
grafana board "checkout-latency", 30-day windows either side of
the 12 Mar deploy. mean barely moved, it was all tail.
nobody had touched it for a year because the real fix needed a
schema change during the freeze — got an exception approved by
the release board, first one that quarter.
me + Sara (she wrote and ran the migration script).
support ticket volume on "cart won't load" dropped too, ask
Marco for that number before I leave.
```

Nothing about that is well written. Lower case, an arrow, a note to self, an unfinished thread. It does not matter. **The note has to be accurate and dated; it does not have to be good.** A capture standard that requires prose is a capture standard you will meet twice.

**What the same person would have written from memory, eighteen months later:**

```text
Improved checkout performance and reduced page load times,
working with the platform team
```

That is the vague-resume failure in one line, and it is what the entire practitioner corpus is complaining about when it says resumes read like job descriptions. Every element that made the work worth describing — the figure, the tail-versus-mean detail, the freeze exception, the year nobody touched it — was available on 14 March and is unavailable now.

**The bullet, written from the note:**

```text
Cut checkout p95 latency from 4.1s to 1.2s by rebuilding the
session lookup, landing the schema change under a release freeze
exception after the fix had been deferred for a year
```

Four rules govern that conversion, and they are the whole technique:

1. **The number never changes between the note and the bullet.** Not rounded up, not restated as a percentage that sounds better, not merged with a second improvement. A figure that grows in transit is the drift described in [patterns/two-document-strategy.md](two-document-strategy.md), and it surfaces in an interview while you are holding the wrong version.
2. **The constraint either becomes the closing clause or gets held back for the interview.** It rarely fits both places. Here the freeze exception earned its space because it explains why the work was hard rather than long.
3. **The "who else" field chooses the verb.** Sara wrote the migration, so this bullet does not claim the migration. What it claims is exactly what its author did.
4. **The source field does not go on the resume.** It stays in the log, and it is what you say when the interviewer asks how you measured it: Grafana, thirty-day windows either side of the deploy, and the mean barely moved.

The sentence shape itself — outcome first, magnitude, then method — is [guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md](../guides/04-bullets-and-evidence.md), which teaches it once and is not restated here.

One mechanical reason the date field earns its place:

> **Vendor-documented (August 2026):** Textkernel's candidate data model attributes each extracted skill to the specific job or education entry it appeared in, and derives from that attribution both the years of experience for that skill and the date it was last used.

An achievement filed under the wrong role puts its skills against the wrong employer and the wrong dates, which is the input to a field recruiters filter on. The date on the note is what prevents that a year later, when two roles have blurred into one.

## 8. Starting from nothing

You cannot backfill five years. You can usually backfill one, and it is worth ninety minutes.

Work through, in this order, and write entries only where you can name a source: your own sent mail, your calendar for the period, commit or ticket history, release notes and launch announcements, chat threads you starred, any performance document that already exists, and — if you contract — the invoices you sent, which are a dated record of exactly what you were paid to deliver.

Two rules for the backfill. **Write the date you can prove, not the date you think it was.** And **leave the number blank rather than guessing it** — an empty field is a prompt to go and look while you still can, whereas an invented one hardens into a claim you will have to defend.

Where a number genuinely cannot be recovered, that is a different problem with its own answer: scale, scope, frequency, duration and breadth are legitimate substitutes for a percentage, and [patterns/quantifying-without-numbers.md](quantifying-without-numbers.md) is the treatment. Do not let the backfill turn into estimation by another name.

Then stop backfilling and start the habit from today. The archaeology is a one-off. The calendar entry is the point.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "I'll remember the important stuff — the big things stick" | The event sticks; the evidence does not. You will remember that you fixed checkout and not that p95 went from 4.1s to 1.2s, and only the second one is worth writing down. |
| "I'll write it all up when I start job hunting" | That is the one moment when the dashboards have rolled over, the accounts are closed and the colleagues have moved on. The material is gathered at the time or not at all. |
| "I keep it in a doc on the company drive" | Offboarding is automated. Your record is deleted or locked on your last day, usually within the hour, and nobody has the authority to make an exception for you. |
| "I saved the link to the dashboard" | The link expires with your badge. Write the value into the note and keep the link only as a convenience. |
| "It felt like bragging, so I stopped" | It is evidence collection with an audience of one. Nothing in the file is shown to anyone until you decide to show it, and the people who feel this most are the ones whose contributions go unrecorded. |
| "I wrote 'improved performance significantly' because I didn't have the number to hand" | You had it that week and you will not have it later. An entry with no number is a reminder to go and get one, not a finished record. |
| "I logged the number but not where it came from" | In the interview you cannot say what was measured, over what period, against what baseline. An indefensible number is worse than no number. |
| "I only log the big wins" | The mid-sized items are what fill out a resume for a role you have not thought of yet, and what your promotion case is actually made of. |
| "I wrote 'led the migration' — realistically it was mostly me" | Record who did what on the day. Reconstructing your share from memory produces the flattering version by default, and an interviewer's second question finds the seam. |
| "My notes are in Slack saves, my calendar, a notebook and two docs" | Four fragments are not a record, because you will consult none of them. Merge them in the first annual pass and keep one file. |
| "I write it up every week without fail — mostly meetings and tickets" | Wrong cadence for your level. Above mid-level the meaningful units resolve over months; a weekly log turns into a diary and gets abandoned for being useless. |
